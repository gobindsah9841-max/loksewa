## 5.1 Main economic indicators (मुख्य आर्थिक सूचक)

#### 1 · Why it matters

Every syllabus has a unit on "economic condition". The examiner's easiest question is a current figure: the growth rate, income per person, the share of agriculture. Questions also test whether you know what the terms mean and which office publishes which figure.

#### 2 · Simple explanation

An **economy** (अर्थतन्त्र) is all the producing, earning and spending of a country. To measure it, we add up what is produced in one year.

- **Gross Domestic Product — GDP** (कुल गार्हस्थ्य उत्पादन): the money value of all final goods and services produced **inside the country** in one year.
- **Gross National Income — GNI** (कुल राष्ट्रिय आय): GDP **plus net income from abroad**, such as wages and interest received from other countries minus those paid out.
- **Gross National Disposable Income — GNDI** (कुल राष्ट्रिय खर्चयोग्य आय): GNI **plus net transfers from abroad**. **Remittance** is the largest transfer. That is why Nepal's GNDI is much larger than its GDP.
- **Per capita** (प्रतिव्यक्ति) means the total divided by the population.
- **Economic growth** (आर्थिक वृद्धि) is the rise in GDP after the effect of price changes has been removed ("at constant prices").
- **Inflation** (मुद्रास्फीति) is a general rise in prices. It is measured by the **Consumer Price Index** (उपभोक्ता मूल्य सूचकाङ्क).

Production is grouped into three sectors:

- **primary** (प्राथमिक) — agriculture, forestry, fishing and mining;
- **secondary** (द्वितीय) — manufacturing, electricity, water supply and construction;
- **tertiary** (तृतीय) or **services** — trade, transport, hotels, finance, real estate, education, health and public administration.

Nepal has a **mixed economy**. The Constitution wants the **public, private and cooperative** sectors to work together and aims at a "socialism-oriented, independent and prosperous economy" (Article 50 (3)). This is often called the **three-pillar economy** (तीन खम्बे अर्थतन्त्र).

#### 3 · Key facts

**Who publishes what**

- **National Statistics Office** (राष्ट्रिय तथ्याङ्क कार्यालय), formerly the Central Bureau of Statistics: the national accounts (GDP), the population census, the living standards survey, the agricultural census and the economic census.
- **Ministry of Finance** (अर्थ मन्त्रालय): the **Economic Survey** (आर्थिक सर्वेक्षण), laid before Parliament just before the budget; the budget speech.
- **Nepal Rastra Bank**: monthly reports on prices, remittance, the balance of payments and foreign-exchange reserves; the monetary policy.
- **Department of Customs** (भन्सार विभाग): export and import figures.
- **National Planning Commission**: the periodic plans.

**The economy in 2082/83** 🔄

The Economic Survey 2082/83 was laid before Parliament on **13 Jestha 2083 (27 May 2026)** by Finance Minister Dr Swarnim Wagle.

- **GDP:** about **Rs 6,609 billion** (66 kharba 9 arba) at current prices.
- **Growth:** **3.85 percent**, a preliminary estimate. The revised figure for 2081/82 is 4.43 percent. The budget for 2082/83 had aimed at 6 percent. The budget for 2083/84 aims at **7 percent**.
- **Per person:** GDP **USD 1,513** (Rs 217,946); GNI **USD 1,535**; GNDI **USD 2,044**.
- **Shares of GDP:** agriculture, forestry and fishing **24.03 percent**; services **61.8 percent**; the rest, about **14 percent**, is industry.
- **Consumption** takes **90.3 percent** of GDP, so domestic saving is under 10 percent.
- **Prices:** average inflation was only 2.13 percent in the first eight months. It rose later and stood at **5.08 percent** year-on-year in mid-July 2026. The budget for 2083/84 wants to hold it within 6 percent; the monetary policy says 5.5 percent.
- **Human Development Index:** **0.622**, rank **145** of 193 countries (Human Development Report 2025) — the "medium human development" group. Life expectancy is **71.3 years**.
- **Income group:** the World Bank has classed Nepal as a **lower-middle-income** country since July 2020. In the United Nations' list Nepal is still a **least developed country** (topic 5.10).

**Provinces** 🔄

- Largest economy: **Bagmati**, 36.7 percent of GDP. Smallest: **Karnali**, 4.2 percent.
- Highest GDP per person: **Bagmati**, USD 2,644. Lowest: **Madhesh**, USD 934.
- Only Bagmati (5.4 percent) and Gandaki (5.01 percent) grew faster than the country as a whole.

**Counting businesses** 🔄

- The first National Economic Census (2075 BS) counted **923,356** establishments with **3,228,457** people working in them.
- The second, the **National Economic Census 2082**, collected its data from 2 Baisakh to 7 Ashad 2083 (15 April to 21 June 2026). Its final results had not been seen when this chapter was checked.

#### 6 · Memory aid

- **"D for Domestic = inside the Door."** GDP counts what is made inside the border.
- **GDP → GNI → GNDI:** each step adds money from abroad — first income, then remittance. **1,513 → 1,535 → 2,044.**
- **"24 – 14 – 62":** farm, factory, services.
- **"Bagmati biggest, Karnali smallest, Madhesh lowest per head."**

#### 7 · Exam traps

- **GDP per person** (USD 1,513) and **GNI per person** (USD 1,535) are both called "per capita income" in guidebooks. Read which one the question names.
- The growth rate of the current year is a **preliminary estimate**. It is revised later. Always attach the fiscal year to the figure.
- **Services**, not agriculture, give the largest share of **GDP**. Agriculture gives the largest share of **employment** (topic 5.2).
- The **Economic Survey** comes from the **Ministry of Finance**, not from Nepal Rastra Bank or the National Planning Commission.
- The Central Bureau of Statistics is now the **National Statistics Office**.
- The smallest economy (Karnali) and the lowest income per person (Madhesh) are different provinces.
- International bodies print other figures for the same idea, because they use other methods and years. Reports of the United Nations' review in 2026 quoted a GNI per person of USD 1,496 for Nepal.

#### 10 · Short-answer question

**Question.** What is meant by GDP and per capita income? Describe the present structure of Nepal's economy.

**Model answer.** **Gross Domestic Product (GDP)** is the money value of all final goods and services produced inside a country in one year. **Per capita income** is the national income divided by the population; it shows the average income of one person. **Structure of Nepal's economy** (estimates for 2082/83): (1) GDP is about Rs 6,609 billion and grew by 3.85 percent; GDP per person is USD 1,513 and GNI per person USD 1,535. (2) **Services** give about 62 percent of GDP, **agriculture** about 24 percent and **industry** about 14 percent, so the economy has moved from farming to services without building a strong industrial base. (3) Agriculture still employs more than half of the workers. (4) Consumption takes about 90 percent of GDP, and it is paid for largely by **remittance**, which also explains why disposable income per person (USD 2,044) is far above GDP per person. (5) Production is unevenly spread: Bagmati alone gives 36.7 percent of GDP and Karnali 4.2 percent. Nepal is therefore a small, consumption-led, remittance-supported economy that needs more production and jobs at home.

## 5.2 Agriculture and land (कृषि र भूमि)

#### 1 · Why it matters

Agriculture gives work to more than half of working Nepalis. Questions on crops and their districts, land measures, land reform and the agricultural census are common in "economic condition" units. Karnali's syllabus names the crops and herbs of the province.

#### 2 · Simple explanation

**Agriculture** (कृषि) covers crops, livestock, forestry and fishing. Most farms in Nepal are small and are worked by the family. They grow food first for the household. This is **subsistence farming** (निर्वाहमुखी कृषि). Output depends heavily on the monsoon, because many fields have no year-round irrigation.

**Land** (भूमि) is the farmer's main asset. The State has therefore made laws on three questions:

- how much land one family may hold — the **ceiling** (हदबन्दी);
- what rights the **tenant** (मोही) has;
- how land may be **used** (भू-उपयोग).

The Constitution's policy on agriculture and land reform (Article 51 (e)) asks for scientific land reform, an end to dual ownership of land, and commercial, modern farming.

#### 3 · Key facts

**Place in the economy** 🔄

- **Share of GDP:** agriculture, forestry and fishing gave **24.03 percent** of GDP in 2082/83.
- **Share of work:** in the census of 2078, **57.3 percent** of employed people worked in agriculture, forestry and fishing. The census of 2068 had given about 65.6 percent.
- **National Agricultural Census, 2078** — the **seventh**; one is taken every ten years:
  - about **4.13 million** farm families;
  - average land per farm family **0.55 hectare** (0.68 hectare in 2068);
  - land operated by farmers **2,218,410 hectares** (2,525,639 hectares in 2068);
  - a woman is the main farmer in **32.4 percent** of farm families.
- **Irrigation:** **1,587,910 hectares** had an irrigation facility by Falgun 2082.
- **Farmland is shrinking:** the Department of Agriculture counts a loss of about 82,000 hectares of cultivable land in six years.

**Crops**

- **Food crops** (खाद्यान्न बाली): paddy (धान), maize (मकै), wheat (गहुँ), millet (कोदो), barley (जौ) and buckwheat (फापर). **Paddy** is the most important, followed by maize and wheat.
- 🔄 **Paddy in 2082/83:** about **5.7 million tonnes**, about 4.2 percent less than the 5.96 million tonnes of 2081/82. This is the Ministry's preliminary estimate as reported; press reports differ on the details.
- **Cash crops** (नगदे बाली): sugarcane, jute, tobacco, tea, coffee, large cardamom, potato, oilseeds and ginger.
- **Tea** was first planted at **Ilam** in 1920 BS (1863). **Coffee** was first planted at Aanpchaur, **Gulmi**, in 1995 BS (1938).
- **Large cardamom** (अलैँची) was the second-largest export item in 2082/83 🔄 (topic 5.3).
- **National Paddy Day** (धान दिवस) is **15 Ashad**.

**Land law**

- **Birta** (tax-free land grants) was abolished in 2016 BS (Chapter 2).
- **Land Act, 2021** (भूमि सम्बन्धी ऐन, २०२१) — the land-reform law that Chapter 2 calls the Land Reform Act. It sets the ceiling and protects tenants. The ceilings today:
  - Terai and Inner Madhesh — **10 bigha**;
  - Kathmandu Valley — **25 ropani**;
  - other hill areas — **70 ropani**.
- The Act allows a little more land for a house plot. To end dual ownership, land tilled by a registered tenant is divided between the landowner and the tenant.
- **Kamaiya** (कमैया), bonded farm labour in the western Terai, was declared ended on **2 Shrawan 2057 (17 July 2000)**.
- **Haliya** (हलिया), bonded ploughmen in the far-western hills, were declared free on **21 Bhadra 2065 (6 September 2008)**.
- **Land Use Act, 2076** (भू-उपयोग ऐन) sorts land into classes such as agricultural, residential, commercial, industrial, forest and public use, so that farmland is not freely cut into house plots.

**Plans and institutions**

- **Agriculture Perspective Plan:** 1995–2015 (20 years).
- **Agriculture Development Strategy:** 2015–2035 (20 years).
- **Prime Minister Agriculture Modernisation Project** (from 2073/74): pocket (10 hectares), block (100), zone (500) and super zone (1,000).
- **Nepal Agricultural Research Council** — 2048 BS. **Agricultural Development Bank** — 2024 BS.

#### 7 · Exam traps

- **Share of GDP** (about 24 percent) and **share of employment** (57.3 percent) are different questions. Old books say "two thirds of the people depend on farming".
- **Bigha** is a Terai measure and **ropani** a hill measure. One bigha is about 13.3 ropani, not 20.
- The present ceiling in the Terai is **10 bigha**. The figure 25 bigha belongs to the original Act of 2021.
- **Kamaiya** (2057) and **haliya** (2065) are different systems, regions and dates.
- **0.55 hectare** is the average per farm family, not per person.
- The **Land Act, 2021** is about ceilings and tenants. The **Land Use Act, 2076** is about what land may be used for.
- Paddy output changes every year 🔄. Quote it with its fiscal year.

#### 10 · Short-answer question

**Question.** Why is the productivity of agriculture low in Nepal? Suggest measures to raise it.

## 5.3 Industry, trade and investment (उद्योग, व्यापार र लगानी)

#### 1 · Why it matters

The trade deficit, the main exports and imports, the classes of industry and the "firsts" of industrial history are stock questions in the economy unit. Current figures on trade appear in current affairs as well.

#### 2 · Simple explanation

**Industry** (उद्योग) turns raw material into goods, or provides an organised service. The law sorts industries by the size of their **fixed capital** (स्थिर पुँजी), because the facilities given by the State differ from class to class.

**Foreign trade** (वैदेशिक व्यापार) is exports (निर्यात) plus imports (आयात).

- The **balance of trade** (व्यापार सन्तुलन) compares the export and import of **goods**. When imports are larger, there is a **trade deficit** (व्यापार घाटा).
- The **balance of payments** (शोधनान्तर स्थिति) is wider. It records **all** money that comes in and goes out: goods, services, remittance, aid, loans and investment.

Nepal has a very large trade deficit. Yet its balance of payments has been in surplus, because remittance from workers abroad pays for the imports.

**Investment** (लगानी) is money put into productive assets. **Foreign direct investment** (प्रत्यक्ष वैदेशिक लगानी) is investment by foreigners in a business in Nepal.

Nepal is **landlocked** (भूपरिवेष्ठित). Its sea trade passes through Indian ports under a transit treaty.

#### 3 · Key facts

**Industry**

- **First steps:** the Industrial Council (उद्योग परिषद्) of 1992 BS, the Company Act of 1993 BS and the **Biratnagar Jute Mill** of 1993 BS (1936), the first modern factory (Chapter 2).
- **Industrial Enterprises Act, 2076** (औद्योगिक व्यवसाय ऐन, २०७६) — the present law. It sorts industries into micro, cottage, small, medium and large (table below).
- By kind of work the Act lists eight groups: energy-based; manufacturing; agriculture- and forest-based; mineral; infrastructure; tourism; information and communication technology; service.
- **Industrial estates** (औद्योगिक क्षेत्र): the oldest is **Balaju** (2016 BS; 1960). Hetauda and Patan followed in 2020 BS. Others are at Dharan, Nepalgunj, Pokhara, Butwal, Bhaktapur, Birendranagar and Rajbiraj. Hetauda is the largest by area.
- **Special Economic Zones** (विशेष आर्थिक क्षेत्र): Special Economic Zone Act, 2073; zones at **Bhairahawa** and **Simara**.
- 🔄 **Companies registered:** 369,646 by Falgun 2082.
- 🔄 **Public enterprises** (सार्वजनिक संस्थान): 45 exist; 27 made a profit and 16 a loss (Economic Survey 2082/83). The Privatisation Act is of 2050 BS.
- 🔄 Industry gives only about 14 percent of GDP. Bagmati produces about a third of the country's industrial output; Karnali about 3 percent.

**Investment**

- **Foreign Investment and Technology Transfer Act, 2075** (वैदेशिक लगानी तथा प्रविधि हस्तान्तरण ऐन).
- **Public–Private Partnership and Investment Act, 2075.**
- **Investment Board Nepal** (लगानी बोर्ड नेपाल): set up in 2068 BS (2011); chaired by the **Prime Minister**; handles projects of **more than Rs 6 billion** and hydropower projects of more than 200 megawatts. Smaller foreign investments are approved by the **Department of Industry**.
- **Minimum foreign investment:** Rs 20 million (2 crore) for each foreign investor, set in 2022; before that it was Rs 50 million.
- 🔄 **2082/83:** the Department of Industry approved foreign-investment commitments of **Rs 58.02 billion**.
- **Nepal Investment Summits:** 2017, 2019 and 2024.

**Foreign trade in 2082/83** 🔄 (Department of Customs, published on 7 Shrawan 2083; 23 July 2026)

- **Exports:** Rs 315.29 billion.
- **Imports:** Rs 2,096.38 billion.
- **Total trade:** Rs 2,411.67 billion — the largest yet.
- **Trade deficit:** Rs 1,781.09 billion.
- For each rupee of exports, Nepal imported **Rs 6.65**.
- **India** is by far the largest partner: imports from India were about Rs 1,210 billion and exports to India about Rs 258 billion — about 61 percent of all trade. China is the second-largest source of imports.
- **Largest export:** **soybean oil**, Rs 128.74 billion — 40.8 percent of all exports. It is refined from imported crude oil and sold to India.
- **Second export:** **large cardamom**, Rs 12.64 billion. Other regular exports: carpets, yarn, garments, jute goods, tea, and iron and steel products.
- **Largest import:** **diesel**, Rs 172.43 billion. Other large imports: crude soybean oil, petrol, cooking gas, vehicles, machinery, iron and steel, and gold.
- **Balance of payments:** a surplus of Rs 1,027.04 billion (Rs 594.54 billion the year before).

**Trade and transit**

- **World Trade Organization:** Nepal became the **147th member** on **23 April 2004 (11 Baisakh 2061)**.
- **South Asian Free Trade Area:** in force from 1 January 2006.
- **Sea access through India:** Kolkata–Haldia and Visakhapatnam.
- **China:** a Transit Transport Agreement was signed in 2016; its protocol of 2019 names four Chinese seaports and three dry ports for Nepal's use.
- **Main customs points:** Birgunj (the largest), Bhairahawa, Biratnagar, Kakarbhitta and Nepalgunj on the Indian border; Rasuwagadhi and Tatopani on the Chinese border. The dry port at Birgunj is linked to the Indian railway.

- A foreign-investment **commitment** is a promise. The money that actually arrives is much less.
- Investment Board Nepal is chaired by the **Prime Minister**, not the Industry Minister.

## 5.4 Tourism (पर्यटन)

#### 1 · Why it matters

Tourism is named with agriculture and energy as a base of Nepal's prosperity, and its figures are easy to examine: the number of tourists, the top source country, the campaign years and their targets, and the bodies that manage the sector.

#### 2 · Simple explanation

**Tourism** (पर्यटन) is travel for holiday, adventure, pilgrimage, study or business, and the services sold to the traveller: transport, hotels, guides, food and entry permits. For Nepal it is an **export of services**: foreigners pay in foreign currency for something that cannot be taken away — mountains, culture, wildlife and places of pilgrimage.

Tourism creates work in remote areas where little else is possible. But it is easily hurt by earthquakes, epidemics, unrest and poor air links.

#### 3 · Key facts

**Numbers** 🔄

- **2025:** **1,158,459** foreign tourists (Nepal Tourism Board) — about 1 percent more than the 1,147,548 of 2024, and about 97 percent of the record **1,197,191** of 2019.
- **Top five countries in 2025:** India 292,438 (25.2 percent); the United States 112,316; China 95,480; the United Kingdom 58,684; Bangladesh 57,545.
- **2026, January to October:** 998,734 — 5.83 percent more than in the same months of 2025.
- **Fiscal year 2082/83:** 1,209,357.
- **Seasons:** autumn (October–November) and spring (March–April) are the busiest. The monsoon months are the quietest.

**Policy and institutions**

- **Ministry of Culture, Tourism and Civil Aviation** — policy.
- **Nepal Tourism Board** (नेपाल पर्यटन बोर्ड): set up in 1998 (2055 BS) as a partnership of the Government and the private tourism industry; it promotes Nepal abroad. Its brand line is "Naturally Nepal — once is not enough".
- **Department of Tourism:** issues permits for mountaineering expeditions, including Sagarmatha.
- **Nepal Mountaineering Association** (1973): manages the smaller "trekking peaks".
- **Tourism Act, 2035; Tourism Policy, 2065.** The first tourism master plan was made in 1972.
- **Nepal Tourism Decade, 2023–2032** (नेपाल पर्यटन दशक) — targets for 2032:
  - **3.5 million** tourists a year;
  - spending of **USD 125** per tourist per day (USD 48 when the plan was made);
  - tourism's share of GDP raised to **10 percent**;
  - **one million** new jobs.

**Campaign years**

- **Visit Nepal Year 1998:** target 500,000; 463,684 came.
- **Nepal Tourism Year 2011:** target one million; 736,215 came; slogan "Together for Tourism".
- **Visit Nepal 2020:** target two million; postponed in March 2020 because of COVID-19.
- **Nepal Tourism Decade:** 2023 to 2032.

**History and kinds of tourism**

- Nepal opened to foreign visitors after the change of 2007 BS. Sagarmatha was first climbed on 29 May 1953. The first organised tour group came in 1955.
- **Mountain tourism:** eight of the world's fourteen peaks above 8,000 metres are in Nepal (Chapter 1).
- **Trekking:** Annapurna, Sagarmatha, Langtang and Manaslu regions.
- **Pilgrimage:** Pashupatinath, Lumbini, Muktinath, Janakpurdham.
- **Wildlife:** Chitwan and Bardiya National Parks.
- **Culture:** the monuments of the Kathmandu Valley. Nepal has four World Heritage Sites (Chapter 1).
- **Village tourism and homestay:** Sirubari in Syangja is known as the first model village.
- **World Tourism Day:** 27 September.

## 5.5 Development infrastructure (विकास पूर्वाधार)

#### 1 · Why it matters

Koshi's unit 1.4 names "development infrastructure" in its title, and every province asks about roads, airports and the "firsts" of transport and communication. New projects — a tunnel, an airport, a railway line — also come up as current affairs.

#### 2 · Simple explanation

**Infrastructure** (पूर्वाधार) means the basic physical systems that production and daily life depend on:

- **transport** — roads, air service, railways, ropeways;
- **energy** — electricity and its transmission lines;
- **communication** — post, telephone, internet;
- **irrigation and drinking water**.

Good infrastructure lowers the cost of moving goods and people, joins markets and carries public services to remote places. In a mountainous, landlocked country it is costly to build and to maintain. Under the Constitution, the Federation builds national highways and large projects, the provinces build provincial roads, and the local levels build local roads (Schedules 5, 6 and 8).

Hydropower is covered in Chapter 1, topic 1.8. This topic gives only the newest figures.

#### 3 · Key facts

**Roads** 🔄 (Economic Survey 2082/83; figures to Falgun 2082)

- Roads of all three levels together: **104,906 km**.
- Road network built by the Federation: **36,806 km** — blacktopped 20,456 km (55.6 percent), gravel 7,602 km, earthen 8,748 km.
- **Longest highway in use:** the Mahendra (East–West) Highway, about 1,028 km (Chapter 1).
- **Tribhuvan Highway:** the first highway to link Kathmandu with the Terai, opened in 2013 BS (1956).
- **Mid-Hill (Pushpalal) Highway** (मध्यपहाडी लोकमार्ग): **1,879 km**, from Chiyo Bhanjyang (Panchthar) to Jhulaghat (Baitadi); 1,510 km blacktopped.
- **Postal Highway** (हुलाकी राजमार्ग): **1,857 km** through the Terai; 1,381 km blacktopped.
- **Kathmandu–Terai Expressway** ("Fast Track"): about 72 km, from Kathmandu to Nijgadh; being built by the Nepali Army.
- **Nagdhunga–Sisnekhola tunnel** 🔄: opened on **11 Shrawan 2083 (27 July 2026)**; main tunnel **2.688 km**; built with Japanese help; described as Nepal's first modern road tunnel. A toll has been charged since 15 Kartik 2083 (1 November 2026).

**Railway, ropeway and cable car**

- **First railway:** Raxaul–Amlekhganj, 1927 (Chapter 2).
- **Janakpur–Jayanagar line:** opened in 1937 as a narrow-gauge line. It has been rebuilt in broad gauge:
  - Jayanagar–Kurtha, about 35 km, opened on 2 April 2022 (19 Chaitra 2078);
  - Kurtha–Bijalpura, about 17 km, opened on 16 July 2023.
- About **52 km** of this line is in use. It is planned to reach Bardibas (68.7 km).
- **Manakamana Cable Car** (1998): the first cable car for passengers.

**Air transport**

- **First aircraft** landed at Gauchar, Kathmandu, in 1949.
- **Royal Nepal Airlines Corporation** (now Nepal Airlines): founded on **1 July 1958 (17 Ashad 2015)**.
- **Civil Aviation Authority of Nepal** (नेपाल नागरिक उड्डयन प्राधिकरण): 31 December 1998.
- 🔄 **International airports in operation: three.**
  - Tribhuvan International Airport, Kathmandu;
  - Gautam Buddha International Airport, Bhairahawa — opened 2 Jestha 2079 (16 May 2022);
  - Pokhara International Airport — opened 17 Poush 2079 (1 January 2023).
- 🔄 **Airports in operation:** 34 in all — the three international airports and 31 domestic ones. Another 18 airports are not in use.
- A fourth international airport is proposed at **Nijgadh**, Bara.

**Communication** 🔄

- **Post:** the first post office, Nepal Hulak Ghar, opened in 1935 BS (1878).
- **Telephone:** first lines in Kathmandu in 1970 BS (1913). Mobile service began in 2056 BS (1999). Internet service began in 1995.
- **Regulator:** Nepal Telecommunications Authority (नेपाल दूरसञ्चार प्राधिकरण), 2054 BS.
- **At the end of 2082/83** (the Authority's annual report): voice telephone density 104.16 percent of the population; mobile broadband 95.09 percent; fixed broadband 53.55 percent.

**Electricity and irrigation** 🔄

- **Access to electricity:** 99.1 percent of the population, counting alternative energy.
- **Installed capacity:** 4,105 megawatts at Falgun 2082 (Economic Survey); about 4,120 megawatts by mid-July 2026 (Chapter 1).
- **Targets:** 5,535 megawatts in the budget for 2083/84; 11,769 megawatts and 700 units of electricity per person by 2085/86 in the Sixteenth Plan.
- **Transmission lines:** 7,280 circuit km.
- **Irrigation:** 1,587,910 hectares with a facility. The Sixteenth Plan aims at about 1.79 million hectares.

**National pride projects** (राष्ट्रिय गौरवका आयोजना)

The Government has named a set of large projects as projects of national pride. They include the Mid-Hill and Postal highways, the Kathmandu–Terai Expressway, the international airports and large irrigation and hydropower schemes.

#### 10 · Short-answer question

**Question.** What is development infrastructure? Describe the present state of transport infrastructure in Nepal and its problems.

**Model answer.** **Development infrastructure** is the set of basic physical systems — transport, energy, communication, irrigation and drinking water — on which production and public services depend. **Present state of transport:** (1) **Roads** are the main means of transport. The three levels of government have built 104,906 km of road; of the 36,806 km federal network, 20,456 km is blacktopped. The Mahendra Highway joins east and west, and the Mid-Hill (1,879 km) and Postal (1,857 km) highways are being completed. The Nagdhunga tunnel opened in Shrawan 2083. (2) **Air:** 34 airports are in operation, three of them international — Kathmandu, Bhairahawa and Pokhara. Air service is the only quick link to many mountain districts. (3) **Rail:** only about 52 km, from Jayanagar to Bijalpura, is in use. **Problems:** many roads are earthen and close in the monsoon; landslides and floods damage them every year; building is slow and costly, and maintenance is neglected; the two new international airports have few international flights; air safety is a concern; and railways and other mass transport are almost absent. **Conclusion:** the country needs to finish the projects it has begun, maintain what is built, and plan roads, air and rail together.

## 5.6 Planned development and the periodic plans (योजनाबद्ध विकास र आवधिक योजना)

#### 1 · Why it matters

Four provinces call the unit "Economic condition and planning". Koshi names "national and provincial periodic plans", and Karnali names "periodic plans". The usual questions ask the period of a plan, the years without a plan, the vision and targets of the plan now running, and who chairs the National Planning Commission.

#### 2 · Simple explanation

A **periodic plan** (आवधिक योजना) sets out what the country wants to achieve in a fixed period of three or five years, how it will do so, and with what money. **Planned development** (योजनाबद्ध विकास) began in Nepal in **2013 BS**.

- The **plan** gives the direction for several years.
- The yearly **budget** gives the money.
- The **Medium-Term Expenditure Framework** (मध्यमकालीन खर्च संरचना) links the two. It looks three years ahead and is renewed every year.

The **National Planning Commission** prepares the plan, the **National Development Council** discusses it, and the **Council of Ministers** approves it. Under federalism each level makes plans for its own work (Article 59), so provinces and local levels also have periodic plans.

#### 3 · Key facts

**Institutions**

- **National Planning Commission** (राष्ट्रिय योजना आयोग):
  - the Government's advisory body for plans, policy, and monitoring and evaluation;
  - a planning body was first formed in **2013 BS (1956)**;
  - chaired by the **Prime Minister**; a full-time **Vice-Chairperson**, members and a member-secretary do the daily work;
  - **not** a constitutional body — it is formed by an order of the Government.
- 🔄 **Vice-Chairperson:** **Dr Gunakar Bhatt**, appointed with six members in Chaitra 2082 (April 2026).
- **National Development Council** (राष्ट्रिय विकास परिषद्): the highest forum for discussing a plan; chaired by the **Prime Minister**.
- **Provinces:** each has a Province Planning Commission or a Province Policy and Planning Commission, normally chaired by the Chief Minister.

**Plans so far**

- **Fifteen** plans have been completed. The **Sixteenth** is running.
- Of the fifteen, **ten were five-year plans** and **five were three-year plans** (the Second, Eleventh, Twelfth, Thirteenth and Fourteenth).
- **Years without a plan** ("plan holiday", योजना बिदा): **2018/19 BS**, after the First Plan; and **2047/48 and 2048/49 BS**, after the Seventh Plan.
- **Landmarks:**
  - **First Plan** (2013–2018): transport and communication came first; the outlay was Rs 33 crore.
  - **Fourth Plan:** the idea of regional development; the country was later divided into development regions.
  - **Seventh Plan:** the basic-needs programme.
  - **Eighth Plan:** the first plan after the restoration of democracy in 2046 BS.
  - **Ninth Plan:** poverty alleviation as the single main objective.
  - **Tenth Plan:** also served as the Poverty Reduction Strategy Paper.
  - **Eleventh Plan:** the Three-Year Interim Plan after the peace agreement.
  - **Fifteenth Plan:** carried the long-term vision to 2100 BS.

**Long-term vision 2100** (दीर्घकालीन सोच २१००)

- Slogan: **"Prosperous Nepal, Happy Nepali"** (समृद्ध नेपाल, सुखी नेपाली).
- Adopted with the Fifteenth Plan; it looks 25 years ahead, to **2100 BS**.
- **Ten national goals:** four of prosperity and six of happiness.
- Aim: income of **USD 12,100** per person by 2100 BS.

**The Sixteenth Plan** (सोह्रौँ योजना)

- **Period:** five years, **2081/82 to 2085/86** (from 16 July 2024 to mid-July 2029).
- **Approved** by the Council of Ministers in Jestha 2081 (May 2024).
- **Vision:** **"Good governance, social justice and prosperity"** (सुशासन, सामाजिक न्याय र समृद्धि).
- **Three objectives:** good governance in every sector; social justice in health, education, employment, housing and public services; prosperity in people's lives and in the national economy.
- **Main strategy:** structural transformation of the economy. The plan has **4 strategies** and names **13 areas** of structural transformation.
- **Estimated investment:** Rs 11,184 billion (111 kharba 84 arba) over the five years.
- **Targets for 2085/86:** growth 7.3 percent; income per person USD 2,351; poverty 12 percent (table below).
- One of the thirteen areas is a smooth graduation from the least-developed-country category and the Sustainable Development Goals (topic 5.10).

#### 10 · Short-answer question

**Question.** Give a short account of planned development in Nepal, and state the vision and main targets of the Sixteenth Plan.

**Model answer.** **Planned development** began in 2013 BS (1956) with the First Five-Year Plan. Since then fifteen plans have been completed — ten five-year plans and five three-year plans — with gaps in 2018/19 and in 2047/48–2048/49. Early plans built roads and communication; the Ninth and Tenth Plans centred on poverty; the Three-Year Interim Plan of 2064/65 followed the peace agreement; and the Fifteenth Plan carried the long-term vision "Prosperous Nepal, Happy Nepali" for 2100 BS. Plans are prepared by the National Planning Commission, which the Prime Minister chairs. **The Sixteenth Plan** (2081/82–2085/86) has the vision **"good governance, social justice and prosperity"**. Its main strategy is structural transformation of the economy in thirteen named areas. **Targets for 2085/86:** economic growth of 7.3 percent; income of USD 2,351 per person; people below the poverty line down to 12 percent; unemployment down to 5 percent; literacy 85 percent; life expectancy 73 years; a Human Development Index of 0.650. **Comment:** most past plans missed their targets, and growth in the Plan's first years has stayed below 5 percent, so success depends on implementation, not on the document.

## 5.7 Budget system and the current budget (बजेट प्रणाली र चालू बजेट)

#### 1 · Why it matters

The budget is the largest economic event of each year. Questions ask its size, who presented it and when, the Articles of the Constitution on public money, and the stages of the budget cycle. Karnali's syllabus names "budget" directly.

#### 2 · Simple explanation

A **budget** (बजेट) is the Government's estimate of its income and spending for one fiscal year, together with its programmes for that year. The Constitution calls it the "estimate of revenue and expenditure" (राजस्व र व्ययको अनुमान).

No tax may be raised and no public money spent except by law (Articles 115 and 117). So the budget must be passed by Parliament. This is how the people's representatives control the Government's purse.

**Spending** is shown under three heads:

- **current expenditure** (चालु खर्च) — salaries, grants and running costs;
- **capital expenditure** (पुँजीगत खर्च) — building assets such as roads and hospitals;
- **financing** (वित्तीय व्यवस्था) — repaying loans and investing in shares and loans.

**Income** comes from **revenue**, **foreign grants** and **loans** (foreign and internal). When spending is more than revenue and grants, the budget is a **deficit budget** (घाटा बजेट), and the gap is filled by borrowing. Nepal's budgets are deficit budgets.

#### 3 · Key facts

**In the Constitution — Part 10, Articles 115 to 125** (federal financial procedures)

- **Article 115:** no tax and no loan except by law.
- **Article 116:** all government revenue goes into the **Federal Consolidated Fund** (संघीय सञ्चित कोष).
- **Article 118:** some spending is **charged** on the Fund and needs no vote of Parliament — for example the pay of the President and Vice-President, of the Chief Justice and Supreme Court judges, of the Speaker and the Chairperson of the National Assembly, of the heads and officials of constitutional bodies and of Province Chiefs, and the Government's debt payments.
- **Article 119:** the **Finance Minister** presents the yearly estimate to a **joint sitting** of both Houses on **15 Jestha**.
- **Article 120:** the Appropriation Act. **Article 121:** supplementary estimates.
- **Article 122:** a **vote on account** (पेस्की खर्च) allows spending in advance while the Appropriation Bill is under discussion — at most **one third** of the year's estimate.
- **Article 123:** a **vote of credit** (उधारो खर्च) for an emergency, when details cannot be given.
- **Article 124:** the **Federal Contingency Fund** (संघीय आकस्मिक कोष) for unforeseen spending.
- **Article 125:** financial procedures are fixed by a federal Act — now the **Financial Procedures and Fiscal Accountability Act, 2076**.
- **Money Bills** start only in the **House of Representatives** and only as Government Bills (Article 110). The National Assembly must return them within **15 days** (Article 111).

**The budget cycle** (बजेट चक्र) — four stages

1. **Formulation** (तर्जुमा): a Resource Committee estimates the resources and fixes ceilings; ministries prepare their programmes within the ceilings; the National Planning Commission and the Ministry of Finance discuss and finalise them.
2. **Approval** (स्वीकृति): Parliament discusses the principles and priorities of the Appropriation Bill at least 15 days before budget day; the Finance Minister presents the budget on 15 Jestha; the Houses debate and pass the Bills; the President authenticates them.
3. **Implementation** (कार्यान्वयन): from 1 Shrawan, authority to spend is sent to the offices; the Financial Comptroller General Office releases money and keeps the accounts; the Ministry of Finance makes a half-yearly review.
4. **Audit and review** (लेखापरीक्षण र मूल्याङ्कन): internal audit during the year; final audit by the **Auditor General** (Article 241); the report goes to the President and Parliament, where the **Public Accounts Committee** examines it.

**Papers of the budget season**

- Before the budget: the **policy and programme** of the Government, read by the President; and the **Economic Survey**.
- On budget day: the **budget speech** (बजेट वक्तव्य); the **Appropriation Bill** (विनियोजन विधेयक) for spending; the **Finance Bill** (आर्थिक विधेयक) for taxes; the **Bill to Raise National Debt** (राष्ट्र ऋण उठाउने विधेयक); and the **Loan and Guarantee Bill** (ऋण तथा जमानत विधेयक).

**History and methods**

- **First budget:** **21 Magh 2008 (4 February 1952)**, presented by Finance Minister **Subarna Shamsher**; about **Rs 5.25 crore**.
- **Budget day** was fixed at 15 Jestha by the Constitution of 2072. The first budget on that day came on 15 Jestha 2073 (28 May 2016).
- **Methods of budgeting:** line-item budget; performance budget; programme budget; **zero-based budget** (every item is justified afresh each year); gender-responsive budget (used in Nepal since 2064/65).

**The budget for 2083/84** 🔄

#### 10 · Short-answer question

**Question.** Describe the budget cycle of the Government of Nepal.

**Model answer.** The budget passes through four stages each year. **(1) Formulation.** A Resource Committee estimates the available resources and fixes ceilings. Each ministry prepares its programmes within its ceiling, guided by the periodic plan and the Medium-Term Expenditure Framework. The National Planning Commission and the Ministry of Finance discuss and finalise the proposals. **(2) Approval.** Parliament first discusses the principles and priorities of the Appropriation Bill. The Finance Minister presents the estimate of revenue and expenditure to a joint sitting on 15 Jestha (Article 119). The Appropriation Bill, the Finance Bill and the borrowing Bills start in the House of Representatives, go to the National Assembly for up to 15 days, and are authenticated by the President before the new fiscal year. **(3) Implementation.** From 1 Shrawan the offices receive authority to spend; the Financial Comptroller General Office releases money from the Federal Consolidated Fund and keeps the accounts; the Ministry of Finance reviews progress at mid-year. **(4) Audit and review.** After internal audit, the Auditor General makes the final audit (Article 241). The report goes to the President and Parliament, and the Public Accounts Committee examines it. The findings feed into the next budget. **Weakness:** the cycle is followed on paper, but capital spending is low — 46.79 percent in 2082/83 — and much of it comes in the last month of the year.

## 5.8 Revenue, taxation and public debt (राजस्व, कर र सार्वजनिक ऋण)

#### 1 · Why it matters

Tax rates, the date and rate of value-added tax, the difference between direct and indirect tax, and the size of the public debt are regular questions. Karnali's syllabus names "revenue jurisdiction". The changes made by the budget for 2083/84 are current affairs for every province.

#### 2 · Simple explanation

The Government gets money in three ways: **revenue**, **grants** and **borrowing**.

- **Revenue** (राजस्व) is **tax revenue** (कर राजस्व) plus **non-tax revenue** (गैरकर राजस्व).
- A **tax** (कर) is a compulsory payment to the State. The payer gets no direct service in return.
- **Non-tax revenue** is other income of the Government: fees, fines, royalties, dividends and interest.

Taxes are of two kinds:

- a **direct tax** (प्रत्यक्ष कर) is borne by the person on whom it is laid — income tax, property tax, vehicle tax;
- an **indirect tax** (अप्रत्यक्ष कर) is collected from a seller or importer but passed on to the buyer in the price — value-added tax, excise duty, customs duty.

A tax is **progressive** (प्रगतिशील) when the rate rises as income rises. Nepal's income tax is progressive.

**Public debt** (सार्वजनिक ऋण) is what the Government owes. **Internal debt** (आन्तरिक ऋण) is owed to lenders inside Nepal. **External debt** (बाह्य ऋण) is owed to foreign governments and international institutions.

#### 3 · Key facts

**Taxes and their laws**

- **Income tax** (आयकर): Income Tax Act, 2058.
- **Value-added tax** (मूल्य अभिवृद्धि कर):
  - Value Added Tax Act, 2052;
  - applied from **1 Mangsir 2054 (16 November 1997)**;
  - it replaced the sales tax and some other taxes;
  - it began at 10 percent; the standard rate is now **13 percent**;
  - **1 Mangsir** is kept as National Tax Day.
- **Excise duty** (अन्तःशुल्क): Excise Duty Act, 2058 — on goods such as liquor, tobacco and vehicles.
- **Customs duty** (भन्सार महसुल): on goods crossing the border.
- **Permanent Account Number** (स्थायी लेखा नम्बर): the nine-digit number of a taxpayer.
- **Four canons of a good tax** (Adam Smith): equity, certainty, convenience and economy.

**Who does what**

- **Inland Revenue Department** (आन्तरिक राजस्व विभाग): income tax, value-added tax and excise.
- **Department of Customs** (भन्सार विभाग): customs duty, and the taxes collected at the border.
- **Department of Revenue Investigation** (राजस्व अनुसन्धान विभाग): leakage of revenue.
- **Revenue Tribunal** (राजस्व न्यायाधिकरण): hears appeals in tax cases.
- **Financial Comptroller General Office** (महालेखा नियन्त्रक कार्यालय): keeps the accounts of revenue and spending.
- **Public Debt Management Office** (सार्वजनिक ऋण व्यवस्थापन कार्यालय): set up in 2075 BS; manages borrowing and its records.

**Which level levies which tax** — see Chapter 4, topic 4.4. In brief: customs, excise, value-added tax and income tax are federal; vehicle tax and agro-income tax are provincial; property tax, land tax, house-rent tax and business tax are local. Value-added tax and domestic excise are shared 70 : 15 : 15.

**Revenue in 2082/83** 🔄 (Financial Comptroller General Office)

- Collected: **Rs 1,241.32 billion** — 83.87 percent of the target of Rs 1,480 billion.
- Tax revenue: Rs 1,121.08 billion. Non-tax revenue: Rs 120.24 billion.
- That is about 18.8 percent of GDP (worked out here from the two figures).
- Target for 2083/84: **Rs 1,405.31 billion**.

**Tax changes from 1 Shrawan 2083 (17 July 2026) — Finance Act, 2083** 🔄

- **Income tax of an individual:** 1 percent up to Rs 10 lakh; 10 percent from Rs 10 to 15 lakh; 20 percent from Rs 15 to 25 lakh; 27 percent from Rs 25 to 40 lakh; **29 percent** above Rs 40 lakh.
- The first band was doubled from Rs 5 lakh (Rs 6 lakh for a couple) to **Rs 10 lakh**. One table now serves single and married taxpayers.
- The 1 percent on the first band is the **social security tax** on salary income.
- The **highest rate** fell from 39 percent to **29 percent**.
- **Value-added tax** stays at 13 percent.
- **Customs tariff bands** were reduced from 11 to 7.
- A **digital service tax** of 2 percent is charged.

**Public debt** 🔄 (Public Debt Management Office)

- Mid-July 2025: Rs 2,674.04 billion.
- **Mid-July 2026: about Rs 2,975 billion** — external Rs 1,599.48 billion (53.77 percent) and internal Rs 1,375.41 billion (46.23 percent); about **45 percent of GDP**.
- **Mid-October 2026: Rs 3,009.11 billion** — 45.59 percent of GDP, and more than Rs 1 lakh for each Nepali.
- **Papers of internal debt:** treasury bills (ट्रेजरी बिल, short-term), development bonds (विकास ऋणपत्र), citizen saving certificates (नागरिक बचतपत्र) and foreign employment saving bonds (वैदेशिक रोजगार बचतपत्र).
- **External lenders:** the World Bank group and the Asian Development Bank are the largest; Japan, India and China are among the lending countries.
- No loan may be raised and no guarantee given except under federal law (Article 115 (2)). Only the Federation may take foreign loans (Chapter 4).

#### 10 · Short-answer question

**Question.** Distinguish between direct and indirect taxes, and state the main sources of the revenue of the Government of Nepal.

**Model answer.** **Direct tax:** the burden falls on the person on whom the tax is laid and cannot be passed on — income tax, property tax, vehicle tax. It can be made progressive, so it is fairer, but it is harder to collect. **Indirect tax:** it is collected from a seller or importer and passed on to the buyer in the price — value-added tax, excise duty, customs duty. It is easy to collect and hard to avoid, but rich and poor pay the same rate. **Sources of revenue.** (1) **Tax revenue**, about nine tenths of the total: value-added tax at 13 percent, customs duty on imports, excise duty, and income tax on persons and companies. Because Nepal imports far more than it produces, a large part of the tax is collected at the border. (2) **Non-tax revenue:** fees, fines, royalties, dividends from public enterprises and interest. In 2082/83 the Government collected Rs 1,241.32 billion — tax Rs 1,121.08 billion and non-tax Rs 120.24 billion — which was 83.87 percent of its target. (3) Besides revenue, the budget is financed by **foreign grants** and by **loans**; public debt reached about Rs 3,000 billion, or about 45 percent of GDP, in 2026. **Conclusion:** revenue depends heavily on imports and consumption, so the base must be widened to production and income at home.

## 5.9 Banking, money and Nepal Rastra Bank (बैंकिङ, मुद्रा र नेपाल राष्ट्र बैंक)

#### 1 · Why it matters

Founding dates of banks, the classes of financial institution, the work of the central bank and the rates in the yearly monetary policy are favourite one-mark questions. The name of the Governor is asked in current affairs.

#### 2 · Simple explanation

A **bank** (बैंक) takes deposits from people who save and lends to people who need money. The **central bank** (केन्द्रीय बैंक) is different: it does not serve the public directly. It issues the currency, keeps the Government's accounts, lends to banks and supervises them. Nepal's central bank is **Nepal Rastra Bank** (नेपाल राष्ट्र बैंक).

Two policies steer the economy:

- **fiscal policy** (वित्त नीति) — the Government's decisions on tax and spending, made through the budget by the Ministry of Finance;
- **monetary policy** (मौद्रिक नीति) — the central bank's decisions on the supply of money, interest and credit, announced once a year and reviewed every quarter.

The central bank's tools include the **bank rate** (the rate at which it lends to banks), the **cash reserve ratio** (the share of deposits a bank must keep with the central bank), the **statutory liquidity ratio** (the share it must keep in liquid assets such as government bonds) and **open market operations** (buying and selling government papers).

#### 3 · Key facts

**Nepal Rastra Bank**

- **Founded:** **14 Baisakh 2013 (26 April 1956)** under the Nepal Rastra Bank Act, 2012.
- **Present law:** Nepal Rastra Bank Act, 2058.
- **First Governor:** Himalaya Shamsher J.B. Rana.
- 🔄 **Present Governor:** **Dr Biswo Nath Poudel**, the 18th, appointed on 6 Jestha 2082 (20 May 2025). The term is five years.
- **Work:** issues notes and coins; makes monetary and foreign-exchange policy; is banker and adviser to the Government; is the bankers' bank and lender of last resort; licenses, regulates and supervises banks and financial institutions; keeps the foreign-exchange reserves; runs the payment system.
- **Aims:** stable prices, a stable balance of payments, a sound financial system and safe payments.

**History of banking**

- **Tejarath Adda**, 1933 BS: a government office that lent to officials; it took no deposits.
- **Nepal Bank Limited**, **30 Kartik 1994 (15 November 1937)**: the first bank.
- **Nepal Industrial Development Corporation**, 2016 BS.
- **Rastriya Banijya Bank**, **10 Magh 2022 (23 January 1966)**.
- **Agricultural Development Bank**, **7 Magh 2024 (21 January 1968)**.
- **Nepal Arab Bank** (now Nabil Bank), 29 Ashad 2041 (12 July 1984): the first joint-venture bank.

**Classes of institution** — Banks and Financial Institutions Act, 2073

- **Class A:** commercial banks (वाणिज्य बैंक) — minimum paid-up capital Rs 8 billion.
- **Class B:** development banks (विकास बैंक).
- **Class C:** finance companies (वित्त कम्पनी).
- **Class D:** microfinance institutions (लघुवित्त वित्तीय संस्था).
- There is also one infrastructure development bank.
- 🔄 **Number in mid-August 2026:** 20 commercial banks, 17 development banks, 16 finance companies, 51 microfinance institutions and 1 infrastructure development bank — **105** in all. A year earlier there were 107.
- Three commercial banks have government ownership: Nepal Bank, Rastriya Banijya Bank and Agricultural Development Bank.

**Money**

- **Currency:** the Nepali rupee; 1 rupee = 100 paisa.
- **First paper notes:** 1 Ashoj 2002 (17 September 1945), issued by the government treasury (सदर मुलुकीखाना).
- **First notes of Nepal Rastra Bank:** 7 Falgun 2016 (19 February 1960).
- **Exchange rate with India:** fixed at **Rs 1.60 for one Indian rupee** since 2049 BS (February 1993). Rates with other currencies change daily.

**Monetary policy for 2083/84** 🔄

- Announced on **23 Ashad 2083 (7 July 2026)** by Governor Dr Biswo Nath Poudel.
- It is the **25th** yearly monetary policy; the first was for 2059/60.
- **Stance:** "cautiously flexible" (सजगतापूर्वक लचिलो).
- **Aims:** to support growth of 7 percent; to hold inflation within **5.5 percent**; to keep reserves enough for at least 7 months of imports; private-sector credit to grow by 11 percent.
- **Rates** (unchanged from the last review):
  - bank rate **5.75 percent** — the top of the interest-rate corridor;
  - policy rate **4.25 percent**;
  - deposit collection rate **2.75 percent** — the floor of the corridor;
  - cash reserve ratio **4 percent**;
  - statutory liquidity ratio **12 percent** for commercial banks and 10 percent for development banks and finance companies.

**External strength in 2082/83** 🔄

- **Foreign-exchange reserves:** **Rs 3,897.67 billion** in mid-July 2026 (Rs 2,677.68 billion a year earlier) — about USD 25.3 billion, enough for more than 19 months of imports of goods and services.
- **Remittance:** Rs 2,363.13 billion (topic 5.10).

**Other parts of the financial system**

- **Securities Board of Nepal** (नेपाल धितोपत्र बोर्ड): 25 Jestha 2050 (7 June 1993); regulates the share market.
- **Nepal Stock Exchange:** began as the Securities Exchange Centre in 2033 BS; trading on its floor began on 29 Poush 2050 (13 January 1994). Its index stood at 2,820.45 in mid-March 2026 🔄.
- **Insurance:** the first insurance company dates from 2004 BS; Rastriya Beema Sansthan from 2024 BS. The regulator is the **Nepal Insurance Authority** (नेपाल बीमा प्राधिकरण) under the Insurance Act, 2079.
- **Employees' Provident Fund** (कर्मचारी सञ्चय कोष): Act of 2019 BS. **Citizen Investment Trust** (नागरिक लगानी कोष): 2047 BS.
- **Social Security Fund** (सामाजिक सुरक्षा कोष): its contribution-based scheme began on 11 Mangsir 2075 (27 November 2018); 31 percent of basic pay is deposited — 11 percent by the worker and 20 percent by the employer.
- **Cooperatives** (सहकारी) 🔄: 32,325 registered by Falgun 2082, holding savings of about Rs 1,030 billion. The Cooperatives Act is of 2074 BS. The first cooperative was formed at Bakhanpur, Chitwan, on **20 Chaitra 2013 (2 April 1957)**.

## 5.10 Poverty, employment, remittance, graduation and the Sustainable Development Goals (गरिबी, रोजगारी, विप्रेषण, स्तरोन्नति र दिगो विकास लक्ष्य)

#### 1 · Why it matters

Koshi's unit 1.10 names the Sustainable Development Goals, and Karnali names them in its environment unit. The poverty rate, the unemployment rate, remittance and Nepal's place in the list of least developed countries are asked everywhere. The decision of October 2026 on graduation is fresh current affairs.

#### 2 · Simple explanation

**Poverty** (गरिबी) is measured in two ways.

- **Absolute poverty** (निरपेक्ष गरिबी): a person is poor when yearly spending is below the **poverty line** (गरिबीको रेखा) — the cost of the food and basic goods a person needs.
- **Multidimensional poverty** (बहुआयामिक गरिबी): it counts what a household lacks in health, education and living standard, not only money.

**Unemployment** (बेरोजगारी) is the share of people in the labour force who want work and cannot find it. Because jobs at home are few, many young Nepalis work abroad. The money they send home is **remittance** (विप्रेषण).

A **least developed country** (अति कम विकसित मुलुक) is a country on a United Nations list of the poorest and most vulnerable states. Such countries get special help in trade and aid. Leaving the list is called **graduation** (स्तरोन्नति).

The **Sustainable Development Goals** (दिगो विकास लक्ष्य) are 17 goals that all member states of the United Nations have agreed to reach by 2030.

#### 3 · Key facts

**Poverty**

- **Fourth Nepal Living Standards Survey, 2079/80** (published on 29 Magh 2080; 12 February 2024):
  - **20.27 percent** of the people — about 5.9 million — live below the poverty line;
  - the poverty line is **Rs 72,908** per person per year;
  - urban areas 18.34 percent; rural areas 24.66 percent;
  - highest in **Sudurpashchim** (34.16 percent); lowest in **Gandaki** (11.88 percent).
- **Earlier surveys:** 41.76 percent (2052/53); 30.85 percent (2060/61); 25.16 percent (2066/67).
- **Multidimensional poverty:** **17.4 percent**.
- **Inequality:** the Gini coefficient of consumption is 0.30.
- By district, poverty runs from about 5.6 percent in Kaski to about 49.6 percent in Achham (small-area estimates published in 2026 🔄).
- **Sixteenth Plan target:** 12 percent by 2085/86.

**Employment**

- **Unemployment:** **12.6 percent** in the survey of 2079/80; 11.4 percent in the Labour Force Survey of 2074/75. Among youth aged 15 to 24 it is 22.7 percent.
- **Minimum wage** 🔄: **Rs 19,550 a month** from 1 Shrawan 2082 (17 July 2025). The Labour Act, 2074 requires a review every two years.
- **Right to employment:** Article 33; Right to Employment Act, 2075. The **Prime Minister Employment Programme** (2075 BS) aims to give registered unemployed people at least 100 days of work a year.
- **Social security allowances** 🔄 reach about 3.64 million people.

**Foreign employment and remittance** 🔄

- Law: Foreign Employment Act, 2064. Offices: Department of Foreign Employment; Foreign Employment Board.
- More than **6.9 million** labour approvals have been issued in all. The main destinations are Malaysia and the Gulf countries — Qatar, Saudi Arabia, the United Arab Emirates and Kuwait.
- **Remittance in 2082/83:** **Rs 2,363.13 billion**, 37.1 percent more than the year before. That is equal to about 36 percent of GDP (worked out here) and more than the whole federal budget.

**Graduation from the least-developed-country category** 🔄

- The United Nations made the list in **1971**. Nepal has been on it from the start.
- **Three criteria**, reviewed every three years by the Committee for Development Policy:
  - Gross National Income per person;
  - the Human Assets Index (health and education);
  - the Economic and Environmental Vulnerability Index.
- A country qualifies when it passes **two of the three** at two reviews in a row.
- Nepal passed the two indices in the reviews of 2015, 2018 and 2021, but not the income test. In **November 2021** the General Assembly decided that Nepal would graduate after five years of preparation, on **24 November 2026**.
- On **13 May 2026** Nepal asked for three more years. It named slow growth, risks to remittance and tourism, climate shocks and the loss of trade preferences.
- On **29 October 2026** the General Assembly adopted a resolution by consensus. It extends the preparation of **Nepal and Bangladesh** to **24 November 2029 (9 Mangsir 2086)**.
- **What graduation changes:** duty-free and quota-free entry for exports, cheap loans and some aid are tied to the list. The European Union, the United Kingdom and Canada keep the trade preference for three more years after graduation.
- Nepal's plan for the change is its **Smooth Transition Strategy**.

**Sustainable Development Goals**

- Adopted by the United Nations General Assembly on **25 September 2015**; they run from **2016 to 2030**.
- **17 goals and 169 targets.** Motto: "Leave no one behind".
- They followed the **Millennium Development Goals** (2000–2015), which had 8 goals.
- In Nepal the **National Planning Commission** leads the work. Nepal has presented voluntary national reviews in 2017, 2020 and 2024.
- 🔄 **SDG Index 2026:** Nepal is **90th of 167** countries with a score of **68.7**. In 2025 it was 85th.
- 🔄 **Human Development Index:** 0.622; rank 145 (topic 5.1).

#### 10 · Short-answer question

**Question.** What is graduation from the least-developed-country category? Why did Nepal ask for more time, and what should it do before 2029?

**Model answer.** The United Nations keeps a list of **least developed countries**, made in 1971, and Nepal has been on it from the start. **Graduation** means leaving the list. It is decided on three criteria — income per person, the Human Assets Index and the Economic and Environmental Vulnerability Index — and a country must pass two of them at two reviews in a row. Nepal passed the two indices, and in 2021 the General Assembly fixed its graduation for 24 November 2026. **Why more time:** on 13 May 2026 Nepal asked for three more years, because growth was weak, remittance and tourism were at risk from conflicts abroad, climate shocks were frequent, and exports such as garments, carpets and pashmina would lose duty-free entry. On 29 October 2026 the General Assembly extended the preparation of Nepal and Bangladesh to **24 November 2029**. **What Nepal should do:** (1) carry out its Smooth Transition Strategy on a fixed timetable; (2) raise production and exports so that trade does not depend on preferences; (3) negotiate new trade arrangements with its main markets; (4) improve roads, power and customs to cut the cost of trade; (5) widen the tax base, because cheap foreign loans and grants will shrink; (6) build skills and jobs at home. **Conclusion:** graduation is a matter of national dignity, but it must be lasting; the three extra years are for preparation, not for delay.
