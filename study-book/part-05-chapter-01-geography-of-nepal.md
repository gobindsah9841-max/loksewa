## 1.1 Location, size and boundaries (अवस्थिति, आकार र सिमाना)

#### 1 · Why it matters

The syllabus begins the geography unit with the geographical situation of Nepal. These facts are fixed, short and easy to test. They also support history (treaties) and international relations (neighbours).

#### 2 · Simple explanation

Nepal is a **landlocked** (भूपरिवेष्ठित) country in South Asia. It has no sea coast. It lies on the southern slope of the Himalaya, between two large neighbours: **China** in the north and **India** in the east, south and west. Its shape is close to a rectangle that stretches from west to east.

#### 3 · Key facts

- **Continent:** Asia. **Region:** South Asia. **Hemispheres:** northern and eastern.
- **Latitude:** 26°22′ N to 30°27′ N. **Longitude:** 80°04′ E to 88°12′ E.
- **Area:** **147,516 sq km**. This is the official figure after the updated political and administrative map of 2077 BS. The earlier figure was 147,181 sq km. The difference is **335 sq km** — the Limpiyadhura–Lipulekh–Kalapani area of Darchula district.
- **Share of land:** about 0.03% of the world and about 0.3% of Asia.
- **Average length (east–west):** 885 km. **Average width (north–south):** 193 km; widest about 241 km, narrowest about 145 km.
- **North:** China (Tibet Autonomous Region). **East, south and west:** India.
- **Five Indian states touch Nepal:** Sikkim and West Bengal (east), Bihar and Uttar Pradesh (south), Uttarakhand (west).
- **Boundary rivers:** the **Mechi** in the east and the **Mahakali** in the west, fixed by the Sugauli Treaty.
- **Nearest sea:** the Bay of Bengal, about 1,127 km away (textbook figure).
- **Nepal Standard Time:** 5 hours 45 minutes ahead of Coordinated Universal Time. It is based on the meridian **86°15′ E**, which passes through **Gaurishankar Himal** in Dolakha.
- **Bangladesh** is separated from Nepal by a narrow strip of India, about 27 km wide. **Bhutan** is separated from Nepal by Sikkim.
- The **Tropic of Cancer** (23°30′ N) does not pass through Nepal. All of Nepal lies north of it.

- **"27 – 15 – 2 – 37":** India 27, China 15, both 2, none 37. Check: 27 + 15 − 2 + 37 = 77.
- **"Karnali has no India. Madhesh and Lumbini have no China."**

- **Five states from east to west:** **S**ikkim, **W**est Bengal, **B**ihar, **U**ttar Pradesh, **U**ttarakhand — "**S**mall **W**ater **B**uffalo, **U**p **U**p".

#### 7 · Exam traps

- **147,181 is the old area.** The current official figure is 147,516 sq km.
- India lies on **three** sides, not only in the south.
- Nepal Standard Time is based on **Gaurishankar**, not Sagarmatha. It is **+5:45**; Indian time is +5:30.
- The Sugauli Treaty was **signed in 1815** and **ratified in 1816**. Read the question's wording.
- Candidates often forget **Sikkim** or **Uttarakhand** when counting the Indian states.
- Boundary lengths differ from source to source ⚖️. Do not treat one figure as beyond question.

#### 10 · Short-answer question

**Question.** Describe the geographical location of Nepal. Give two advantages and two difficulties that come from it.

**Model answer.** Nepal lies in South Asia between 26°22′ N and 30°27′ N and between 80°04′ E and 88°12′ E. It is a landlocked country of 147,516 sq km, with China to the north and India on the other three sides. **Advantages:** (1) it lies between two very large markets and can act as a bridge for trade and transit; (2) its position on the Himalayan slope gives it great variety of climate, water resources and scenery for hydropower and tourism. **Difficulties:** (1) with no sea coast, foreign trade depends on transit through a neighbour, which raises cost and time; (2) the mountain terrain makes roads and services costly and exposes the country to earthquakes, landslides and floods.

## 1.2 Physiographic regions (भौगोलिक प्रदेश)

#### 1 · Why it matters

The syllabus names "physical divisions" and "geographical diversity and way of life". Questions ask for the share of each region, the order of the ranges, and the features of each belt.

#### 2 · Simple explanation

Nepal rises like a staircase from south to north. In less than 200 km the land climbs from about 60 m above sea level to 8,848.86 m. The belts run west to east. The simple division has three belts: **Terai** (plain), **Pahad** (hill) and **Himal** (mountain). A more detailed division has five.

#### 3 · Key facts

**Three-fold division (traditional)**

- **Himal (mountain):** about **15%** of the area. Land above about 3,000 m. Permanent snow begins at about 5,000 m (the snow line).
- **Pahad (hill):** about **68%** of the area. It includes the Mahabharat range, the midland valleys and the Chure hills.
- **Terai (plain):** about **17%** of the area. A flat, fertile strip of alluvial soil, the northern edge of the Ganga plain. It is called the **granary** (अन्नको भण्डार) of Nepal.

**Five-fold division (Land Resource Mapping Project)**

- Terai 13.7% · Churia (Siwalik) 12.8% · Middle Mountains 29.2% · High Mountains 20.4% · High Himal 23.9%.

**Ranges and belts from south to north**

1. **Main Terai** — plain.
2. **Bhawar** — a narrow belt of gravel and sand at the foot of the Chure; water sinks underground here.
3. **Chure (Siwalik) hills** — low hills, mostly between about 600 m and 1,800 m. The youngest and weakest hills, made of loose sand, gravel and soft rock.
4. **Inner Terai (भित्री मधेश) or Dun valleys** — flat valleys between the Chure and the Mahabharat: Udayapur, Sindhuli, Makawanpur, Chitwan, Nawalpur, Dang, Deukhuri, Surkhet.
5. **Mahabharat range (Mahabharat Lek)** — higher hills, mostly between about 1,500 m and 3,000 m.
6. **Midlands** — hills, river basins, flat river terraces (टार) and valleys such as Kathmandu and Pokhara.
7. **Great Himalaya** — the main snow range.
8. **Inner Himalaya (Bhot) valleys** and the **marginal mountains** on the Tibetan side — dry lands such as Mustang, Manang and Dolpa.

**How the land was made**

- The Himalaya are **young fold mountains**. They formed when the Indian plate pushed against the Eurasian plate and lifted the bed of the old **Tethys Sea**.
- The mountains are still rising, so Nepal is an earthquake-prone country.
- Three great faults run west to east. From north to south: **Main Central Thrust**, **Main Boundary Thrust**, **Main Frontal Thrust**.

**Other facts**

- **Dang** is known as the largest valley of Nepal.
- The **Kathmandu Valley** is the bed of an old lake.
- The Chure is protected through the President Chure–Terai Madhesh Conservation Development Board, formed in 2071 BS (2014).

#### 6 · Memory aid

- **Area "15 – 68 – 17", people "6 – 40 – 54"** (Himal – Pahad – Terai). The Terai has little land but the most people.
- **Districts "16 – 40 – 21".** 16 + 40 + 21 = 77.
- **Faults from north to south: C – B – F** (Central, Boundary, Frontal): "**C**ome **B**ack, **F**riend".
- **South to north:** Terai → Bhawar → Chure → Dun → Mahabharat → Midland → Himal.

#### 7 · Exam traps

- The Terai has **17% of the land but more than half of the people**.
- **Chure and Mahabharat are different ranges.** The Chure is lower, younger and lies to the south.
- **Dun valleys** (Chitwan, Dang, Surkhet) lie between the Chure and the Mahabharat. They are not part of the main Terai.
- **Kathmandu and Pokhara are midland valleys**, not Dun valleys.
- Two systems give two sets of percentages. "68% hill" belongs to the three-fold system; "29.2% Middle Mountains" belongs to the five-fold system.

#### 10 · Short-answer question

**Question.** Compare the three geographical regions of Nepal by area, population and economic use.

**Model answer.** The **Himal** covers about 15% of the land but holds only about 6% of the people. It is cold and steep; people depend on animal herding, potato and barley, medicinal herbs and tourism. The **Pahad** covers about 68% of the land and holds about 40% of the people. Its mild climate suits terrace farming, fruit, tea, hydropower and tourism; most old towns and the capital lie here. The **Terai** covers only about 17% of the land but holds about 54% of the people. Its flat alluvial soil, roads and nearness to the Indian border make it the centre of food-grain production, industry and trade. The three belts depend on each other: water and power flow from north to south, and food and goods move from south to north.

## 1.3 Mountains, passes and glaciers (हिमाल, भन्ज्याङ र हिमनदी)

#### 1 · Why it matters

Peaks, their heights and districts, passes and glaciers are classic matching and ordering questions. Glaciers and glacial lakes also connect with the environment unit (climate change).

#### 2 · Simple explanation

The Himalaya run for about 2,400 km across Asia. About 800 km — one third — lie in Nepal. Of the world's 14 peaks higher than 8,000 m, **eight** stand in Nepal or on its border. A **pass** (भन्ज्याङ; in Tibetan, *La*) is a low point in a range that people use to cross it. A **glacier** (हिमनदी) is a slow river of ice. A **glacial lake** (हिमताल) forms where the ice melts behind a wall of loose rock.

#### 3 · Key facts

- **Sagarmatha (Mount Everest)** is **8,848.86 m** high. Nepal and China announced this height together on **23 Mangsir 2077 (8 December 2020)**. The older figure was 8,848 m.
- Names of Sagarmatha: *Sagarmatha* (Nepali; the name is credited to the historian Baburam Acharya), *Chomolungma* (Tibetan), *Everest* (after Sir George Everest), *Peak XV* (survey name).
- First ascent of Sagarmatha: **16 Jestha 2010 (29 May 1953)** by **Tenzing Norgay Sherpa** and **Edmund Hillary**. 29 May is observed as International Sagarmatha Day.
- The first 8,000-metre peak ever climbed was **Annapurna I**, on 3 June 1950, by a French team led by Maurice Herzog.
- **Dhaulagiri, Manaslu and Annapurna** lie wholly inside Nepal. The highest of them is **Dhaulagiri I (8,167 m)**.
- **Machhapuchhre** ("Fishtail", 6,993 m, Kaski) is not open for climbing.
- **Glaciers:** an inventory by the International Centre for Integrated Mountain Development (ICIMOD) counted **3,808 glaciers** in Nepal.
- **Glacial lakes:** a 2020 study counted **2,070 glacial lakes** in Nepal. It listed 47 potentially dangerous lakes in the Koshi, Gandaki and Karnali basins, **21 of them in Nepal**.
- Well-known glacial lakes: **Tsho Rolpa** (Dolakha), **Imja** (Solukhumbu), **Thulagi** (Manang).
- A **glacial lake outburst flood** happens when the wall of a glacial lake breaks. Examples: Dig Tsho, Solukhumbu (4 August 1985); Thame, Solukhumbu (16 August 2024).
- 🔄 **Bhote Koshi flood, 10 Bhadra 2083 (26 August 2026):** a mass of ice and rock fell into the Lhende stream above Rasuwagadhi, and a flood of debris ran down the Bhote Koshi and the Trishuli. It is one of the deadliest disasters in Nepal's history: more than 1,450 people dead and about 5,300 missing in early November 2026. Scientists were still studying the exact cause. Chapter 7, topic 7.10 gives the details.
- The United Nations named **2025** the International Year of Glaciers' Preservation, and **21 March** the World Day for Glaciers.
- Nepal held the first **Sagarmatha Sambaad** (dialogue) in Kathmandu on 16–18 May 2025, on the theme "Climate Change, Mountains and the Future of Humanity".

- **Thorong La** is not a border pass.

#### 10 · Short-answer question

**Question.** What is a glacial lake outburst flood? Why is the risk growing in Nepal, and what can be done?

**Model answer.** A glacial lake outburst flood is a sudden flood that occurs when the natural wall of ice or loose rock holding a glacial lake breaks. **Why the risk grows:** rising temperature melts glaciers faster, so lakes grow larger; the walls are weak moraine; avalanches, earthquakes or heavy rain can trigger a break. Nepal has 2,070 glacial lakes, of which 21 are listed as potentially dangerous. **What can be done:** (1) regular monitoring by satellite and field survey; (2) lowering the water level, as was done at Tsho Rolpa and Imja; (3) early-warning systems for villages downstream; (4) hazard mapping before building roads and power plants; (5) raising the issue of climate justice in international forums, because Nepal contributes very little to global emissions.

## 1.4 Rivers and lakes (नदी र ताल)

#### 1 · Why it matters

The syllabus names natural resources and geographical diversity; the Lumbini syllabus names rivers, lakes and glacial lakes directly. River systems give ordering, matching and odd-one-out questions.

#### 2 · Simple explanation

Almost every river of Nepal flows from north to south, joins the Ganga in India and ends in the Bay of Bengal. Rivers are grouped by where they begin. Rivers that begin in the snow of the Himalaya flow all year. Rivers that begin in the Mahabharat range also flow all year but shrink in winter. Rivers that begin in the Chure hills run only in the rainy season and bring sudden floods.

#### 3 · Key facts

- Nepal has **more than 6,000** rivers and streams.
- **Three large river systems:** Koshi (east), Gandaki (centre), Karnali (west). The **Mahakali** is the fourth snow-fed river and forms the western boundary.
- **Koshi** — the **largest** river of Nepal. Called *Saptakoshi* because of its seven branches. The **Arun** is its largest branch; it rises in Tibet and is older than the Himalaya it cuts through. The Koshi is known as the "Sorrow of Bihar".
- **Gandaki** — called *Saptagandaki* in the hills and **Narayani** in the plain. The Kali Gandaki cuts a very deep gorge between Dhaulagiri and Annapurna. Sacred *shaligram* stones are found in the Kali Gandaki. Textbooks name the Narayani as the **deepest** river of Nepal.
- **Karnali** — the **longest** river of Nepal (**507 km** inside Nepal). It rises in Tibet near Lake Mansarovar and enters Nepal in Humla. It leaves Nepal through the Chisapani gorge.
- **Mahakali** — the western boundary river. Nepal holds that it rises at Limpiyadhura.
- **Bagmati** — rises at Bagdwar on Shivapuri hill and drains the Kathmandu Valley through the Chobhar gorge.
- **Lakes:** an official inventory counts **5,358 lakes** in Nepal.
- **Rara** (Mugu) is the **largest** lake: about 10.8 sq km, at 2,990 m.
- **Phoksundo** (Dolpa) is the textbook answer for the **deepest** lake (145 m) ⚖️.
- **Tilicho** (Manang, 4,919 m) is the textbook answer for the **highest** lake ⚖️.
- **Phewa** (Kaski) is the best-known lake of Pokhara. **Indra Sarovar** (Kulekhani, Makawanpur) is a man-made lake.

## 1.5 Climate, soil, natural vegetation and forest (हावापानी, माटो, वनस्पति र वन)

#### 1 · Why it matters

"Climate" (हावापानी) and "natural resources" are named in every syllabus read. This topic also feeds the environment unit, which carries three more questions.

#### 2 · Simple explanation

Height decides climate in Nepal. A short journey from the Terai to the snow line passes through almost every climate on earth, from hot plain to frozen waste. Rain comes mainly in summer with the **monsoon** (मनसुन) wind from the Bay of Bengal. Soil and plants change with the climate, so each belt has its own crops and forests.

#### 3 · Key facts

**Climate**

- Climate is controlled by **altitude**, latitude, the monsoon wind, the direction a slope faces, and distance from the sea.
- The summer monsoon comes from the **Bay of Bengal** and enters Nepal **from the east**. Rain therefore decreases from east to west.
- About **80%** of the year's rain falls in the monsoon months, June to September.
- **Normal date of monsoon onset: 13 June. Normal date of withdrawal: 2 October.** These are the dates used by the Department of Hydrology and Meteorology.
- 🔄 In 2026 the monsoon entered on 19 June (5 Ashad 2083) and left the whole country on 14 October (28 Ashoj 2083), twelve days later than normal.
- **Winter rain** comes with westerly winds from the Mediterranean side. It enters from the west, so the west gets more of it.
- **Lumle** (Kaski) receives the most rain in Nepal, more than 5,000 mm a year. **Mustang** and **Manang** lie in the **rain shadow** north of Annapurna and Dhaulagiri and receive very little.

**Soil**

- **Alluvial soil** (पाँगो माटो) — Terai and river valleys. The most fertile.
- **Sandy and gravelly soil** — Chure and Bhawar. Poor for crops; suited to forest.
- **Red, brown and rocky soil** — Mahabharat and the hills. Suited to terrace farming, tea and fruit.
- **Lacustrine soil** (तलैया माटो) — the Kathmandu Valley, the bed of an old lake. Very fertile.
- **Glacial or mountain soil** — the Himalayan belt. Stony and the least fertile.

**Forest**

- 🔄 **Forest cover:** land-cover figures published by the Forest Research and Training Centre in 2024 give **forest 43.38%** and **other wooded land 2.70%**, together **46.08%** of the country.
- The earlier national figure (Forest Resource Assessment, 2015) was forest 40.36% and other wooded land 4.38%, together 44.74%.
- By the 2015 assessment, the forest is spread as follows: Middle Mountains 37.80%, High Mountains and High Himal 32.25%, Churia 23.04%, Terai 6.90%.
- **Community forestry** (सामुदायिक वन) hands the management of national forest to local user groups. Nepal has more than 22,000 such groups 🔄.
- The **Forest Act, 2076** provides for government-managed forest, forest conservation areas, community forest, partnership forest, leasehold forest, religious forest and private forest.

Lumle and Pokhara receive very heavy rain because the Annapurna range rises steeply behind them, while Mustang and Manang, behind the range, stay dry.

## 1.6 Protected areas and biodiversity (संरक्षित क्षेत्र र जैविक विविधता)

#### 1 · Why it matters

This topic serves two units at once: geography of Nepal (natural resources) and ecosystem and environment (biodiversity, rare species). Lists of parks, their districts and their special animals are ideal for matching questions.

#### 2 · Simple explanation

A **protected area** is land set aside by law to save wild plants, animals and landscapes. Nepal has four kinds. A **national park** (राष्ट्रिय निकुञ्ज) protects a whole natural area. A **wildlife reserve** (वन्यजन्तु आरक्ष) protects particular animals and their home. A **hunting reserve** (शिकार आरक्ष) allows controlled, licensed hunting. A **conservation area** (संरक्षण क्षेत्र) protects nature while local people continue to live and work there. A **buffer zone** (मध्यवर्ती क्षेत्र) is the belt of villages and forest around a park, where local people share in the park's income.

#### 3 · Key facts

- Nepal has **20 protected areas**: **12 national parks, 1 wildlife reserve, 1 hunting reserve and 6 conservation areas**. Around them are **13 buffer zones**.
- Together they cover **23.39%** of the country (34,419.75 sq km, including buffer zones). The share is worked out on the earlier area of 147,181 sq km.
- The law is the **National Parks and Wildlife Conservation Act, 2029 (1973)**. The Department of National Parks and Wildlife Conservation was set up in 2037 BS (1980).
- **First national park:** Chitwan (1973). **Largest national park:** Shey Phoksundo. **Smallest national park:** Rara.
- **Largest protected area of any kind:** Annapurna Conservation Area. **Smallest:** Krishnasar (Blackbuck) Conservation Area.
- **Shuklaphanta and Parsa** were wildlife reserves until 2017, when they became national parks. **Koshi Tappu** is now the only wildlife reserve.
- **Dhorpatan** is the only hunting reserve.
- Annapurna, Manaslu and Gaurishankar Conservation Areas are managed by the National Trust for Nature Conservation. Kanchenjunga Conservation Area is managed by a local council.
- **World Heritage Sites (natural):** Sagarmatha National Park (1979) and Chitwan National Park (1984). The cultural sites are the Kathmandu Valley (1979) and Lumbini (1997).
- **Ramsar sites** (wetlands of international importance): **10**. The first was Koshi Tappu (1987).
- **Biodiversity:** Nepal has 118 ecosystems and 35 forest types. On about 0.1% of the world's land it holds about 3.2% of the world's known plants and 1.1% of its animals.
- 🔄 **Tiger:** **429** (national count announced on 29 July 2026). The count of 2022 had found 355.
- 🔄 **One-horned rhinoceros:** 752 (count of 2021).
- 🔄 **Snow leopard:** 397 (first national estimate, announced in April 2025).
- The **spiny babbler** (काँडे भ्याकुर) is the only bird found nowhere but Nepal.

#### 10 · Short-answer question

**Question.** What is a buffer zone? How does it help conservation?

**Model answer.** A buffer zone is the area of villages, farms and forest around a national park or reserve, declared under the National Parks and Wildlife Conservation Act. Nepal has 13 buffer zones. **How it helps:** (1) a share of the park's income, 30 to 50 per cent, goes back to local communities for development and conservation work; (2) user groups manage buffer-zone forests, so people can meet their need for firewood and fodder outside the park; (3) people who gain from the park help to guard it against poaching and fire; (4) relief and protective measures reduce conflict between people and wild animals; (5) the zone gives wild animals extra space around the core area. In short, the buffer zone turns local people from outsiders into partners of the park.

## 1.7 Administrative geography and superlatives (प्रशासनिक भूगोल र विशेषता)

#### 1 · Why it matters

Numbers of provinces, districts and local levels, and "largest / smallest" facts, are tested directly. The Koshi syllabus prints a model question on the number of local levels in the province. This topic is also the base for all seven province chapters.

#### 2 · Simple explanation

Under the Constitution of 2072 BS Nepal is a federal state with three levels: the **federation** (संघ), the **provinces** (प्रदेश) and the **local levels** (स्थानीय तह). There are 7 provinces, 77 districts and 753 local levels. A local level is a metropolitan city, a sub-metropolitan city, a municipality or a rural municipality. Each local level is divided into wards.

#### 3 · Key facts

- **7 provinces · 77 districts · 753 local levels · 6,743 wards.**
- **753 local levels** = **6** metropolitan cities (महानगरपालिका) + **11** sub-metropolitan cities (उपमहानगरपालिका) + **276** municipalities (नगरपालिका) + **460** rural municipalities (गाउँपालिका).
- **Metropolitan cities (6):** Kathmandu, Lalitpur, Bharatpur, Pokhara, Biratnagar, Birgunj.
- **Sub-metropolitan cities (11):** Itahari, Dharan, Janakpurdham, Kalaiya, Jitpur Simara, Hetauda, Butwal, Ghorahi, Tulsipur, Nepalgunj, Dhangadhi.
- **Pokhara** is the largest metropolitan city by area. **Kathmandu** is the largest by population.
- The Constitution divided two old districts between provinces, raising the number from 75 to 77: **Nawalparasi** became Nawalpur (Gandaki) and Nawalparasi West (Lumbini); **Rukum** became Rukum East (Lumbini) and Rukum West (Karnali).
- The five development regions and fourteen zones no longer exist.
- **Largest district:** Dolpa (7,889 sq km). **Smallest district:** Bhaktapur (119 sq km).
- **Most populous district:** Kathmandu (2,041,587). **Least populous:** Manang (5,658).
- **Census of 2078 BS** (census day 9 Mangsir 2078, 25 November 2021): population **29,164,578**; male 14,253,551; female 14,911,027; **198** persons per sq km; yearly growth **0.92%**; 6,666,937 households.
- ⚖️ **Lowest point:** the traditional textbook answer is **Kechana Kawal** in Jhapa, quoted at about 58 to 70 m. More recent reports name **Mukhiyapatti Musharniya** in Dhanusha (about 59 m) as the lowest point. Neither version could be confirmed from an official source during this research, so learn both.

## 1.8 Hydropower and natural resources (जलविद्युत् र प्राकृतिक स्रोत)

#### 1 · Why it matters

"Natural resources" stands in the geography unit, and electricity stands in the economy unit as development infrastructure. Capacity figures change every year, so this topic also feeds current affairs.

#### 2 · Simple explanation

Nepal's steep slopes and snow-fed rivers can produce a great deal of electricity. Most of Nepal's power plants are **run-of-river** plants: they use the river's flow as it comes, so they produce most in the monsoon and least in the dry winter. A **storage** plant keeps water in a reservoir and can produce power when it is needed. Besides water, Nepal's natural resources are forest, land, minerals and its scenery.

#### 3 · Key facts

**Water and electricity**

- **Theoretical hydropower potential:** about **83,000 MW**. About **42,000–43,000 MW** is considered technically and economically feasible.
- **First hydropower station:** **Pharping**, 500 kW, opened in **1911 (1968 BS)**.
- **First storage project:** Kulekhani I (60 MW, 1982), in Makawanpur.
- **Largest station now operating:** **Upper Tamakoshi**, 456 MW, in Dolakha, operating since 2021 🔄.
- The **Nepal Electricity Authority** was set up in 2042 BS (1985).
- 🔄 **Installed capacity:** about **4,120 MW** at the end of fiscal year 2082/83 (mid-July 2026). This is a press figure quoting the Nepal Electricity Authority. The Bhote Koshi flood of 10 Bhadra 2083 (26 August 2026) then damaged 13 hydropower projects of about 759 MW in all, some working and some still being built (the Electricity Authority's own count was 14 projects and 748 MW).
- 🔄 **Government target:** 28,500 MW by 2035 (Energy Development Roadmap).
- **Electricity trade:** Nepal first sold power in India's power exchange in November 2021. A long-term agreement for India to buy 10,000 MW over ten years was signed on 4 January 2024. Nepal first sent power to **Bangladesh** — 40 MW through India's grid — on **15 November 2024**.
- The **Dhalkebar** sub-station in Dhanusha is the Nepal end of the 400 kV Dhalkebar–Muzaffarpur line, the main cross-border power line.

**Irrigation and multipurpose projects**

- **Sunkoshi–Marin Diversion:** carries water from the Sunkoshi through a tunnel to the Marin stream in Sindhuli and on to the Bagmati, to irrigate land in **Bara, Rautahat, Sarlahi, Mahottari and Dhanusha**.
- **Bheri–Babai Diversion:** carries water from the Bheri to the Babai through a tunnel, to irrigate land in Banke and Bardiya.
- Other large irrigation projects: **Sikta** (Banke), **Babai** (Bardiya), **Rani Jamara Kulariya** (Kailali), **Mahakali** (Kanchanpur).

**Minerals**

- Nepal has many kinds of minerals, but few are mined on a large scale. **Limestone**, used for cement, is the most important.
- 🔄 **Natural gas in Dailekh:** exploration drilling at Jaljale, with Chinese assistance, reached 4,013 m. The final report, made public in February 2026, puts the methane reserve at about **80.7 billion cubic metres**. Whether it can be produced commercially has not yet been tested.

#### 10 · Short-answer question

**Question.** "Nepal is rich in water resources but poor in their use." Explain in brief.

**Model answer.** Nepal has more than 6,000 rivers and a theoretical hydropower potential of about 83,000 MW, of which about 42,000 MW is feasible. Yet installed capacity reached only about 4,120 MW by mid-2026, and much farmland still depends on rain. **Reasons:** (1) large projects need heavy investment and long construction time; (2) difficult terrain, weak roads and transmission lines; (3) most plants are run-of-river, so power is surplus in the monsoon and short in the dry season; (4) delays from land acquisition, forest clearance and contract management; (5) dependence on agreements with neighbours for markets and for rivers shared with them. **Way forward:** build storage projects, complete transmission lines inside the country and across the border, raise domestic use of electricity in industry, cooking and transport, secure long-term export markets, and use the same water for irrigation and drinking water.

## 1.9 Map-based General Knowledge (नक्सामा आधारित सामान्य ज्ञान)

#### 1 · Why it matters

Level II questions ask you to arrange places in order or match a place with its district. You can answer them only if you carry a picture of the map in your head. This topic builds that picture in words.

#### 2 · Simple explanation

Think of Nepal as a long rectangle with **three strips** from south to north (Terai, Pahad, Himal) and **seven blocks** from east to west (the provinces). Madhesh is the exception: it is a thin strip along the southern border. Koshi lies to its east and north-east, and Bagmati to its north and west. Then hang the rivers, peaks, parks and towns on this frame, always from east to west.

#### 6 · Memory aid

- **Three strips, seven blocks.** South to north: Terai, Pahad, Himal. East to west: provinces 1 to 7, with number 2 (Madhesh) tucked along the south.
- **Madhesh, east to west: "Sa – Si – Dha – Ma – Sar – Rau – Ba – Pa"** (Saptari, Siraha, Dhanusha, Mahottari, Sarlahi, Rautahat, Bara, Parsa).
- **Big rivers, east to west: K – B – N – K** (Koshi, Bagmati, Narayani, Karnali), with the boundary rivers Mechi and Mahakali at the two ends.
- **Always fix the two ends first.** In an ordering question, the easternmost and westernmost items usually leave only one option.

#### 7 · Exam traps

- **Madhesh does not stretch from south to north** like the other provinces. It is a southern strip only: Koshi lies to its east and north-east, Bagmati to its north and west.
- **Two Godawaris:** the capital of Sudurpashchim is in Kailali; the botanical garden is in Lalitpur.
- **Lumbini the birthplace is in Rupandehi.** Tilaurakot, the old palace site, is in Kapilvastu. Ramgram is in Nawalparasi West.
- **Simraungadh and Gadhimai are both in Bara.**
- **Jumla, Kalikot and Bajura do not touch China**, although they are mountain districts.
- **Dhading touches China** with a short stretch, and so does **Bajhang**. Candidates often leave them out.
- Several headquarters are called **Khalanga** (Jumla, Jajarkot, Pyuthan, Salyan, Darchula). Read the district name.

### D. Five short-answer questions

**1. Explain the geographical diversity of Nepal and its effect on the way of life.**
*Points for the answer:* a rise from about 60 m to 8,848.86 m within about 193 km · three belts with different climate, soil and plants · Himal: herding, trade, tourism, scattered villages · Pahad: terrace farming, mixed crops, old towns · Terai: grain farming, industry, dense settlement · effect on food, clothing, houses and festivals · difficulty for roads and services, but advantage for hydropower, tourism and biodiversity.

**2. Describe the major river systems of Nepal and their economic importance.**
*Points for the answer:* Koshi, Gandaki and Karnali systems, with the Mahakali on the western boundary · seven branches of the Koshi and of the Gandaki · snow-fed, so they flow all year · hydropower, irrigation, drinking water, fertile soil, fishing, rafting, religious sites · agreements with India on the Koshi, Gandak and Mahakali · floods, erosion and the need for river control.

**3. Why is the Chure region being damaged? What are the effects, and what should be done?**
*Points for the answer:* young, loose rock · forest cutting, settlement, grazing, digging of sand, gravel and stone · effects: landslides, flash floods, rising river beds, loss of farmland and drying of water sources in the Terai · measures: control of extraction, forest restoration, river-bank protection, alternative livelihoods, coordination among the three levels of government.

**4. Discuss the role of protected areas and buffer zones in conserving biodiversity.**
*Points for the answer:* 20 protected areas and 13 buffer zones on 23.39% of the land · protection of habitats from the Terai to the Himal · recovery of the tiger and the rhinoceros · income from tourism · sharing of 30 to 50 per cent of income with local people · problems: conflict between people and wildlife, poaching, roads and other construction, climate change.

**5. Assess Nepal's hydropower potential, present position and problems.**
*Points for the answer:* about 83,000 MW in theory and about 42,000 MW feasible · about 4,120 MW installed by mid-2026 · mostly run-of-river, so surplus in the monsoon and shortage in the dry season · export to India and, since 2024, to Bangladesh · problems of investment, transmission lines, storage and markets · need for storage projects and higher domestic use.

### E. Common mistakes

1. Writing **147,181 sq km** as the present area.
2. Saying India lies only to the **south** of Nepal.
3. Linking Nepal Standard Time with **Sagarmatha** instead of Gaurishankar.
4. Giving the Terai **the largest area** because it has the most people.
5. Calling **Kanchenjunga** or **Sagarmatha** the highest peak "wholly inside Nepal".
6. Mixing **largest river** (Koshi) with **longest river** (Karnali).
7. Counting **three wildlife reserves**; there is now one.
8. Mixing **largest national park** (Shey Phoksundo) with **largest protected area** (Annapurna).
9. Giving **Madhesh** the largest population, or **Madhesh** the most local levels.
10. Naming **Butwal** or **Dhangadhi** as a provincial capital.
11. Quoting **83,000 MW** as the usable hydropower potential.
12. Quoting a forest, electricity or wildlife figure **without its year**.
