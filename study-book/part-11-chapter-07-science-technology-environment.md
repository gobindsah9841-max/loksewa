## 7.1 Science in daily life (दैनिक जीवनमा विज्ञान)

#### 1 · Why it matters

The first line of every science unit is "the direct effect of the development of science and technology on human life". Questions come from units of measurement, instruments, inventors and simple facts about heat, light, sound and electricity. They suit matching questions very well.

#### 2 · Simple explanation

**Science** (विज्ञान) is knowledge gained by watching, measuring and testing. **Technology** (प्रविधि) is the use of that knowledge to make tools and solve problems. Science explains why water boils; technology gives us the pressure cooker.

Science and technology have changed every part of life — health, food, travel, communication and work. They have also brought new problems: weapons, pollution, electronic waste and loss of privacy. The exam asks for both sides.

#### 3 · Key facts

**Measurement**

- The international system of units (SI) has **seven base units**: metre (length), kilogram (mass), second (time), ampere (electric current), kelvin (temperature), mole (amount of substance) and candela (brightness of a light source).
- Units made from them: **newton** (force), **joule** (work and energy), **watt** (power), **pascal** (pressure), **hertz** (frequency), **volt** (electric potential difference), **ohm** (resistance), **coulomb** (electric charge).
- Special units: **light year** — distance, not time; **nautical mile** — distance at sea, 1.852 km; **knot** — speed of ships; **fathom** — depth of water; **decibel** — loudness of sound; **horsepower** — power, 746 watts; **kilowatt-hour** — electrical energy, the "unit" on an electricity bill; **carat** — purity of gold, 24 carat being pure.

**Force and pressure**

- **Newton's three laws of motion:** a body stays at rest or in steady motion unless a force acts on it (inertia); force equals mass times acceleration; every action has an equal and opposite reaction.
- **Gravity** pulls every body towards the earth. A falling body gains about 9.8 metres per second of speed every second. On the moon a body weighs about one sixth of its weight on earth; its mass does not change.
- **Air pressure** falls as height rises. So water boils below 100 °C in the mountains and food cooks slowly. A **pressure cooker** raises the pressure, so the water boils at a higher temperature and food cooks fast.

**Heat**

- Heat travels in three ways: **conduction** (through solids), **convection** (through liquids and gases) and **radiation** (without any medium — the sun's heat reaches us this way).
- Water freezes at 0 °C = 32 °F = 273 K and boils at 100 °C = 212 °F = 373 K. Normal body temperature is 37 °C = 98.6 °F. The Celsius and Fahrenheit scales show the same number at −40°.
- Water is densest at 4 °C. That is why ice floats and fish survive under a frozen lake.

**Light**

- Light travels at about **300,000 km per second**. Sunlight takes about **8 minutes 20 seconds** to reach the earth.
- **Reflection** — light bounces back (mirror). **Refraction** — light bends when it enters another medium (a stick looks bent in water). **Dispersion** — white light splits into seven colours (rainbow; violet, indigo, blue, green, yellow, orange, red). **Scattering** — makes the sky blue and the sunset red. **Total internal reflection** — used in optical fibre; it also causes the mirage and the sparkle of a diamond.
- The primary colours of light are **red, green and blue**.
- **Lenses:** a convex lens corrects long sight and is used as a magnifying glass; a concave lens corrects short sight.
- **Mirrors:** a convex mirror is the rear-view mirror of a vehicle; a concave mirror is used by dentists, in torches and for shaving.

**Sound**

- Sound needs a medium. It cannot travel through a vacuum. It travels fastest in solids, slower in liquids and slowest in gases — about 332 metres per second in air at 0 °C.
- The human ear hears from **20 hertz to 20,000 hertz**. Sound above that is **ultrasound** (bats; medical scans; SONAR for measuring the depth of the sea). Sound below 20 hertz is infrasound.
- An **echo** is reflected sound.

**Electricity**

- Metals are good conductors; rubber, dry wood, glass and plastic are insulators.
- **Ohm's law:** voltage = current × resistance.
- A **fuse** is a thin wire of low melting point. It melts and breaks the circuit when too much current flows.
- House supply in Nepal is alternating current at about 230 volts and 50 hertz.
- LED bulbs give the same light with far less electricity than filament bulbs.

#### 7 · Exam traps

- A **light year** is a unit of **distance**.
- **Mass** stays the same on the moon; **weight** falls.
- Sound travels **fastest in solids**, not in air.
- A **convex** mirror, not a concave one, is the rear-view mirror.
- The **kilowatt-hour** measures **energy**, not power. The watt measures power.
- Water boils at a **lower** temperature on a mountain.
- Edison did not invent the telephone; Bell did.

#### 10 · Short-answer question

**Question.** How has the development of science and technology affected human life? Give both good and bad effects.

**Model answer.** Science is knowledge gained by observation and experiment; technology is its practical use. **Good effects:** (1) **Health** — vaccines, antibiotics and modern surgery have controlled many diseases and raised the length of life; in Nepal life expectancy has reached 71.3 years. (2) **Food** — improved seed, fertiliser and irrigation have raised production. (3) **Transport and communication** — roads, aircraft, the mobile phone and the internet have made the world small; services can be given online. (4) **Energy and industry** — electricity and machines have made work faster and lighter. (5) **Safety** — satellites and warning systems give early notice of storms and floods. **Bad effects:** (1) pollution of air, water and soil, and climate change from burning coal and petroleum; (2) weapons of mass destruction; (3) cybercrime, false information and loss of privacy; (4) loss of jobs to machines, and electronic waste; (5) a lazy way of life and new diseases such as diabetes and high blood pressure. **Conclusion:** science is a tool. Its results depend on how people use it, so its use must be guided by law, ethics and care for the environment.

## 7.2 Chemistry in daily life: soap, plastic, fibre, glass, fertiliser and pesticide (दैनिक जीवनमा रसायन: साबुन, प्लास्टिक, रेसा, काँच, मल र कीटनाशक)

#### 1 · Why it matters

The syllabi of Gandaki, Koshi, Lumbini and Bagmati name these six materials one by one: "soap, plastic, fibre, glass, fertiliser and pesticide". No other science sub-unit is so exact, so these six are the safest marks in the unit.

#### 2 · Simple explanation

Everything around us is made of chemicals. An **element** (तत्त्व) has only one kind of atom — iron, oxygen, gold. A **compound** (यौगिक) joins two or more elements — water, salt. The six materials of the syllabus are all man-made compounds or mixtures that we use every day. For each one, learn four things: what it is made of, its kinds, its use, and its harm.

#### 3 · Key facts

**Basic chemistry**

- **118 elements** are known. Dmitri Mendeleev made the first periodic table in 1869. The modern table is arranged by atomic number.
- An atom has protons and neutrons in its nucleus and electrons around it.
- **Acids** taste sour and turn blue litmus red. **Bases** feel soapy and turn red litmus blue. The **pH scale** runs from 0 to 14: 7 is neutral, below 7 is acid, above 7 is base.
- **Air** is about 78 percent nitrogen, 21 percent oxygen and 0.93 percent argon; carbon dioxide is about 0.04 percent.
- **Rusting** is the slow joining of iron with oxygen in the presence of water. Coating iron with zinc to stop rust is called galvanising.
- **Alloys:** brass = copper + zinc; bronze = copper + tin; steel = iron + carbon; stainless steel = iron + chromium + nickel.
- Mercury is the only metal that is liquid at room temperature. Diamond, a form of carbon, is the hardest natural substance.

**1. Soap (साबुन)**

- Soap is the **sodium or potassium salt of a fatty acid**.
- It is made by heating fat or oil with an alkali. The process is **saponification**; the by-product is **glycerol** (glycerine).
- **Sodium** soaps are hard — washing soap. **Potassium** soaps are soft — toilet soap, liquid soap, shaving cream.
- A soap molecule has a head that loves water and a tail that loves oil. The tails catch the dirt and the water carries it away.
- Soap gives little lather in **hard water**; it forms a scum.
- A **detergent** is a similar cleaner, usually made from petroleum products. It works well even in hard water. Some detergents do not break down in nature, and their phosphates feed weeds in ponds.

**2. Plastic (प्लास्टिक)**

- Plastic is a **polymer** — a very long chain made by joining many small molecules (monomers). Most plastics are made from petroleum products.
- **Thermoplastics** soften when heated and can be shaped again: polythene, PVC (polyvinyl chloride), polystyrene, polypropylene, PET, nylon.
- **Thermosetting plastics** cannot be softened again once set: **Bakelite** (electric switches, handles), melamine (unbreakable plates).
- Bakelite, made by Leo Baekeland in 1907, was the first fully man-made plastic.
- Teflon is the coating of non-stick pans. PVC is used for pipes.
- **Harm:** plastic does not rot; it chokes drains and soil, kills animals that eat it, and gives poisonous smoke when burnt. Tiny pieces called microplastics are now found in water and food.
- **Remedy — the three Rs:** reduce, reuse, recycle.

**3. Fibre (रेसा)**

- **Natural fibres** come from plants (cotton, jute, hemp, allo, flax) or from animals (wool, silk, pashmina).
- Cotton is almost pure cellulose. Wool and silk are proteins. Silk comes from the cocoon of the silkworm; rearing silkworms is called sericulture.
- **Man-made fibres** are of two kinds. **Rayon**, made from the cellulose of wood pulp, is called **artificial silk**. **Synthetic fibres** are made wholly from chemicals: **nylon** (ropes, parachutes, brushes, stockings), **polyester** or terylene (clothing), **acrylic** (artificial wool).
- **Nylon**, made in 1935 by Wallace Carothers, was the first fully synthetic fibre.
- Synthetic fibres are strong, light and dry fast, but they melt near fire and do not absorb sweat.

**4. Glass (काँच)**

- Glass is made by melting **silica (sand)** with **soda ash (sodium carbonate)** and **limestone (calcium carbonate)**.
- Glass has no regular crystal form; it is called a **supercooled liquid**.
- **Kinds:** soda-lime glass — the ordinary soft glass of windows and bottles; hard (potash) glass — laboratory ware; **borosilicate glass** (Pyrex) — stands heat, used for laboratory and kitchen ware; **lead (flint) glass** — lenses, prisms and decorative ware; safety glass — vehicle windscreens; photochromic glass — spectacles that darken in sunlight.
- **Colour** comes from metal oxides: cobalt gives blue, chromium green, manganese purple.
- Hydrofluoric acid eats into glass and is used to write on it.
- Glass can be recycled again and again.

**5. Fertiliser (मल)**

- Plants need three main nutrients: **nitrogen (N)** for leaves and growth, **phosphorus (P)** for roots, flowers and seeds, and **potassium (K)** for strength and resistance to disease. Together they are called **NPK**.
- **Chemical fertilisers:** **urea** — about **46 percent nitrogen**, the richest common nitrogen fertiliser; ammonium sulphate — about 21 percent nitrogen; **DAP** (di-ammonium phosphate) — 18 percent nitrogen and 46 percent phosphate; **muriate of potash** — about 60 percent potash.
- **Organic manures:** farmyard manure, compost, vermicompost (made by earthworms) and green manure (a crop such as dhaincha ploughed into the soil).
- **Biofertilisers** are living things that feed the soil: *Rhizobium* bacteria in the root nodules of peas and beans fix nitrogen from the air; blue-green algae and azolla do the same in paddy fields.
- **Harm of overuse:** the soil turns sour and hard; nitrates enter drinking water; fertiliser washed into ponds makes weeds grow and fish die.
- 🔄 **Nepal makes no chemical fertiliser.** All of it is imported. Two public enterprises — Krishi Samagri Company Limited and Salt Trading Corporation Limited — bring in and sell the subsidised fertiliser. By February 2026 about 483,600 tonnes had been imported in fiscal year 2082/83; the ministry puts the yearly need for subsidised fertiliser at about 800,000 tonnes.

**6. Pesticide (कीटनाशक विषादी)**

- A pesticide is a chemical that kills or controls pests.
- **Kinds by target:** insecticide — insects; fungicide — fungi; herbicide or weedicide — weeds; rodenticide — rats and mice.
- **DDT** was first used as an insecticide in 1939 by Paul Müller. It stays in soil and water for years and gathers in the bodies of birds, fish and people. Most countries have banned it in farming.
- **Bordeaux mixture** — copper sulphate with lime — is an old fungicide.
- **Bhopal gas tragedy:** on the night of 2–3 December 1984, the poisonous gas methyl isocyanate leaked from a pesticide factory in Bhopal, India, and killed thousands.
- **Harm:** poisoning of farmers; residue in vegetables; death of bees, birds and fish; pests that become resistant.
- **Safe use:** use only when needed and in the right dose; wear a mask and gloves; wait the stated number of days between spraying and harvest; wash vegetables well.
- **Integrated pest management** uses many methods together — resistant seed, traps, friendly insects, plant-based sprays such as neem — and chemicals only as the last step.
- Nepal's law is the **Pesticide Management Act, 2076**.

## 7.3 Cell, genes and evolution (कोष, वंशाणु र क्रमविकास)

#### 1 · Why it matters

"Evolution and genes" is written in the science unit of every syllabus that could be read. The questions are about a few names (Mendel, Darwin, Lamarck, Watson and Crick), a few numbers (46 chromosomes) and a few terms (vestigial organ, natural selection).

#### 2 · Simple explanation

Every living thing is made of **cells** (कोष). Inside the nucleus of a cell are thread-like **chromosomes**. They carry **genes** (वंशाणु), the units that pass characters from parents to children. Genes are made of the chemical **DNA**.

Small changes in genes add up over a very long time, and living things slowly change. This slow change is **evolution** (क्रमविकास). **Heredity** explains why children are like their parents; evolution explains why life on earth has so many forms.

#### 3 · Key facts

**The cell**

- The cell is the basic unit of the structure and work of life.
- **Robert Hooke** saw and named the cell in 1665, in a thin slice of cork.
- **Schleiden and Schwann** gave the cell theory in 1838–39: all plants and animals are made of cells.
- **Robert Brown** discovered the nucleus in 1831.
- Parts and their work: **nucleus** — control centre; **mitochondrion** — "powerhouse", releases energy; **ribosome** — makes protein; **chloroplast** — makes food in green plants; **cell membrane** — controls what goes in and out.
- A **plant cell** has a cell wall of cellulose and chloroplasts. An **animal cell** has neither.
- Bacteria have no true nucleus. They are called prokaryotes.
- The largest single cell is the egg of the ostrich. The longest cell in the human body is the nerve cell.

**Genes and heredity**

- **Gregor Mendel**, an Austrian monk, is the **father of genetics**. He worked on the **pea plant** and published his laws of heredity in 1866.
- A normal human body cell has **46 chromosomes — 23 pairs**. Of these, 22 pairs are alike in men and women; the last pair are the **sex chromosomes**.
- A woman has **XX**; a man has **XY**. The mother always gives an X. The father gives an X or a Y, so **the father's sperm decides the sex of the child**.
- **DNA** (deoxyribonucleic acid) has the shape of a twisted ladder — the **double helix**. **James Watson and Francis Crick** described it in **1953**.
- A sudden change in a gene is a **mutation**.
- **Inherited disorders:** colour blindness and haemophilia (carried on the X chromosome, so more common in men); Down syndrome (one extra chromosome — 47 in all); sickle-cell anaemia and thalassaemia (disorders of the blood).
- The **Human Genome Project**, which read the whole human DNA, was completed in 2003.
- **DNA fingerprinting**, used to identify persons and prove parentage, was developed by Alec Jeffreys in 1984.

**Evolution**

- **Jean-Baptiste Lamarck** (1809): organs grow with use and shrink with disuse, and such changes pass to the young — the "inheritance of acquired characters". His example was the long neck of the giraffe. Science does not accept this theory now.
- **Charles Darwin** (1859, *On the Origin of Species*): **natural selection**. More young are born than can survive; they differ a little from one another; those best fitted to their surroundings live and breed; over a long time new species arise. Darwin gathered his evidence on the voyage of the ship *Beagle*.
- **Hugo de Vries** (1901): new species can arise by sudden changes — the mutation theory.
- **Evidence of evolution:**
  - **fossils** — remains of old life preserved in rock;
  - **homologous organs** — same structure, different work: the human arm, the wing of a bat, the flipper of a whale;
  - **analogous organs** — different structure, same work: the wing of an insect and the wing of a bird;
  - **vestigial organs** — organs that no longer have a use: the **appendix**, the wisdom teeth, the tailbone;
  - **connecting links** — *Archaeopteryx*, a fossil with characters of both reptiles and birds.
- Life is believed to have begun in water.
- **Human evolution** in short: early apes → *Australopithecus* → *Homo habilis* (tool maker) → *Homo erectus* (upright man, user of fire) → Neanderthal man → *Homo sapiens* (modern man).
- A fossil tooth of the early ape ***Ramapithecus*** was found on the bank of the **Tinau river near Butwal**. It is among the oldest evidence of ape life in Nepal.

#### 10 · Short-answer question

**Question.** What is evolution? Explain Darwin's theory of natural selection and give the evidence for evolution.

**Model answer.** **Evolution** is the slow, continuous change by which simple forms of life have developed into the many complex forms of today. **Darwin's theory of natural selection** (1859) has these steps: (1) living things produce more young than can survive; (2) so there is a struggle for food, space and safety; (3) the young differ a little from one another — variation; (4) those whose variations suit the surroundings survive and breed — "survival of the fittest"; (5) useful variations pass to the next generation and, over a very long time, add up to new species. **Evidence of evolution:** (a) **fossils** show that old forms were simpler and that forms have changed in order; (b) **homologous organs**, such as the human arm and the bat's wing, show a common origin; (c) **vestigial organs**, such as the appendix, are remains of organs once useful; (d) **connecting links** such as *Archaeopteryx* join two groups; (e) the early stages of the young of fish, birds and mammals look alike. Later science has added the knowledge of genes and mutation to Darwin's theory.

## 7.4 Health, disease, nutrition, vaccines and sanitation (स्वास्थ्य, रोग, पोषण, खोप र सरसफाइ)

#### 1 · Why it matters

"Health, disease, vaccine, food, nutrition, sanitation" stands in every science unit. Bagmati goes further, with a whole unit on "education and health" that names infant, child and maternal mortality, malnutrition, health services, yoga and exercise, epidemics and COVID-19. Health figures are 🔄 facts; the names of vitamins and germs are not.

#### 2 · Simple explanation

The World Health Organization defines **health** (स्वास्थ्य) as "a state of complete physical, mental and social well-being and not merely the absence of disease". A **disease** (रोग) is any condition that stops the body or mind from working well.

Diseases are of two kinds. A **communicable disease** (सरुवा रोग) passes from one person to another through germs — tuberculosis, cholera, COVID-19. A **non-communicable disease** (नसर्ने रोग) does not pass on — diabetes, cancer, heart disease. Nepal now carries both loads at once.

Good health rests on five things: enough of the right **food**, clean **water and sanitation**, **vaccines**, a healthy **way of life**, and **health services** that people can reach and afford.

#### 3 · Key facts

**Germs and the diseases they cause**

- **Virus:** common cold, influenza, COVID-19, measles, rubella, polio, rabies, dengue, hepatitis, AIDS, chickenpox, mumps, Japanese encephalitis.
- **Bacterium:** tuberculosis, typhoid, cholera, leprosy, tetanus, diphtheria, whooping cough, plague, pneumonia.
- **Protozoan:** malaria, kala-azar, amoebic dysentery.
- **Fungus:** ringworm.
- **Worm:** roundworm, hookworm, elephantiasis (filariasis).

**Carriers (vectors)**

- Female **Anopheles** mosquito — malaria.
- **Aedes** mosquito — dengue and chikungunya; it bites in the daytime and breeds in clean standing water.
- **Culex** mosquito — Japanese encephalitis and filariasis.
- **Sandfly** — kala-azar.
- Rat flea — plague. Infected dog — rabies. Housefly — carries the germs of diarrhoea, typhoid and cholera to food.

**Water-borne diseases:** cholera, typhoid, diarrhoea, dysentery, hepatitis A and E.

**Non-communicable diseases**

- The four main groups are diseases of the **heart and blood vessels**, **cancers**, **long-term lung diseases** and **diabetes**.
- Their common causes are tobacco, alcohol, unhealthy food, lack of exercise and polluted air.
- **Diabetes** arises when the pancreas makes too little **insulin** or the body cannot use it.
- Normal blood pressure of an adult is about **120/80**.

**Food and nutrition**

- **Carbohydrates** give energy (rice, maize, potato). **Proteins** build the body (pulses, milk, egg, meat). **Fats** give stored energy. **Vitamins and minerals** protect the body. Water and fibre are also needed.
- A **balanced diet** (सन्तुलित भोजन) has all of these in the right amounts.
- Vitamins **A, D, E and K** dissolve in fat; vitamins **B and C** dissolve in water. Sunlight on the skin makes vitamin D.
- **Three measures of child malnutrition:** **stunting** — too short for the age (long hunger); **wasting** — too thin for the height (recent hunger); **underweight** — too light for the age.
- Children from 6 months to 5 years get **vitamin A capsules** twice a year in Nepal. Iodised salt prevents goitre.

**Vaccines**

- A vaccine (खोप) trains the body to fight a germ before the real germ arrives.
- **Edward Jenner** made the first vaccine, against smallpox, in 1796. **Louis Pasteur** made the rabies vaccine in 1885. **Jonas Salk** made the injected polio vaccine in 1955; Albert Sabin made the oral one.
- **Alexander Fleming** discovered penicillin, the first antibiotic, in 1928. **Robert Koch** found the germ of tuberculosis in 1882.
- **Smallpox** is the only human disease wiped out from the whole world (1980).
- Nepal's **National Immunisation Programme** began in **2034 BS** (1977/78). All its vaccines are free.

**Diseases Nepal has defeated** 🔄

- **Polio:** the World Health Organization certified its South-East Asia Region, which includes Nepal, polio-free on **27 March 2014**.
- **Maternal and neonatal tetanus:** eliminated in 2005.
- **Leprosy:** eliminated as a public-health problem at the national level in 2010.
- **Trachoma** (an eye disease that causes blindness): eliminated in **2018**.
- **Rubella:** on **18 August 2025** the World Health Organization announced that Nepal had **eliminated rubella as a public-health problem** — the sixth country of the Region to do so.
- **Not yet:** Nepal aimed to eliminate **measles** by 2026, but measles broke out in several districts in 2026 while vaccine was short. Kala-azar, filariasis and malaria are close to their targets but had not been declared eliminated when this chapter was checked.

**Health in the Constitution and the law**

- **Article 35:** every citizen has the right to **free basic health services**; no one may be denied emergency health services; every citizen has the right of access to clean drinking water and sanitation.
- **Article 30:** the right to live in a clean and healthy environment.
- Public Health Service Act, 2075. National Health Policy, 2076. Health Insurance Act, 2074.
- 🔄 The ministry is now the **Ministry of Health and Food Security**.

**Health services**

- **Bir Hospital**, opened in 1947 BS, is the oldest hospital of Nepal.
- Health posts and basic health service centres in the wards; basic hospitals at the local level; provincial hospitals; federal hospitals and health academies.
- **Female Community Health Volunteers** (महिला स्वास्थ्य स्वयंसेविका) carry health messages, vitamin A and family-planning supplies to every village.
- **Safe motherhood programme** (आमा सुरक्षा कार्यक्रम): free delivery in health institutions and a transport allowance.
- 🔄 **Health insurance:** begun in Kailali on **25 Chaitra 2072 (7 April 2016)**. A family of up to five pays **Rs 3,500 a year** and gets treatment worth up to **Rs 100,000**; each further member adds Rs 700 and Rs 20,000 of cover, up to Rs 200,000. Citizens aged 70 and above are insured free. Patients of eight listed serious diseases get a further Rs 100,000. In Shrawan 2083 about **5.97 million** people were insured, and joining online had begun. The Board has decided that members aged 16 and above must show the national identity card from 1 Poush 2083.

**COVID-19** (Bagmati names it)

- The disease is caused by the virus **SARS-CoV-2**. It was first reported from **Wuhan, China**, in December 2019.
- The World Health Organization named it COVID-19 on 11 February 2020 and declared a **pandemic on 11 March 2020**. It ended the world health emergency on 5 May 2023.
- **Nepal:** the first case was confirmed on **9 Magh 2076 (23 January 2020)** in a student who had returned from Wuhan — the first case in South Asia. The first nationwide lockdown began on **11 Chaitra 2076 (24 March 2020)**. The first death, of a young mother from Sindhupalchok, came in mid-May 2020. Vaccination began on **14 Magh 2077 (27 January 2021)**.
- An **epidemic** is a disease that spreads fast in one area; a **pandemic** spreads across countries and continents; an **endemic** disease stays in one area all the time.

**Yoga, exercise and moral education** (Bagmati names them)

- **Yoga** (योग) joins body, breath and mind. Patanjali's *Yoga Sutra* sets out **eight limbs**: yama, niyama, asana, pranayama, pratyahara, dharana, dhyana and samadhi.
- **Asana** is posture; **pranayama** (प्राणायाम) is control of the breath; **dhyana** (ध्यान) is meditation.
- **International Day of Yoga: 21 June**, marked since 2015. Nepal also marks a **National Yoga Day on 1 Magh**.
- The World Health Organization advises adults to take at least **150 minutes of moderate exercise a week**.
- Moral education teaches honesty, discipline, respect and care for others — the base of a healthy society.

**Water and sanitation**

- 🔄 Nepal was declared an **open-defecation-free country on 13 Ashoj 2076 (30 September 2019)**.
- 🔄 The Economic Survey 2082/83 puts **basic drinking-water service at about 97 percent** of the people, but **safe (higher-standard) service at only about 29 percent**.
- Ways to make water safe at home: boiling, filtering, chlorine, and leaving clear bottles in strong sunlight.
- Washing hands with soap is the cheapest way to stop diarrhoea.

- Insulin comes from the **pancreas**, not the liver.
- Nepal's first COVID-19 **case** was in January 2020; the first **lockdown** was in March 2020.

## 7.5 Information and communication technology and cyber security (सूचना तथा सञ्चार प्रविधि र साइबर सुरक्षा)

#### 1 · Why it matters

"Information and communication technology" is named by every syllabus that could be read. Bagmati adds "the internet and the computer as means of communication". Karnali names electronic service delivery and cyber security. Chapter 4 (topic 4.8) explained e-governance; this topic gives the technology behind it and the safety rules.

#### 2 · Simple explanation

**Information and communication technology** (सूचना तथा सञ्चार प्रविधि) is the set of tools that store, process and send information — the computer, the mobile phone, the internet, radio, television and satellites.

A **computer** takes data in (input), works on it (process), keeps it (storage) and gives results (output). The **internet** is a worldwide network of computer networks.

**Cyber security** (साइबर सुरक्षा) is the protection of computers, networks and data from theft, damage and misuse. A crime done with or against a computer or network is a **cybercrime**.

#### 3 · Key facts

**The computer**

- **Charles Babbage** is called the father of the computer. **Ada Lovelace** is called the first programmer.
- **Generations by main part:** first — vacuum tube; second — transistor; third — integrated circuit; fourth — microprocessor; fifth — artificial intelligence.
- **Hardware** is the machine you can touch; **software** is the set of instructions.
- The **CPU** (central processing unit) is the brain of the computer.
- **Input devices:** keyboard, mouse, scanner, microphone. **Output devices:** monitor, printer, speaker.
- **Memory:** RAM holds data only while the power is on; ROM keeps it.
- A computer works in the **binary** system of 0 and 1. The smallest unit is the **bit**; **8 bits make 1 byte**; 1,024 bytes make 1 kilobyte; then megabyte, gigabyte and terabyte.
- An **operating system** runs the machine: Windows, Linux, Android.

**The internet**

- The internet grew from **ARPANET**, a network of the United States defence department, begun in **1969**.
- **Tim Berners-Lee** invented the **World Wide Web** in **1989**. The web is one service on the internet; the two are not the same thing.
- **Ray Tomlinson** sent the first e-mail in 1971.
- Terms: **URL** — address of a web page; **HTTP / HTTPS** — rules for sending web pages (the "S" means secure); **IP address** — number of a device on a network; **ISP** — company that sells internet service; **Wi-Fi** and **Bluetooth** — wireless links; **browser** — program for viewing web pages; **search engine** — tool for finding pages.
- Newer terms: **cloud computing** — using storage and programs kept on distant computers; **Internet of Things** — everyday objects linked to the internet; **artificial intelligence (AI)** — machines doing work that needs thinking, a term first used by John McCarthy in 1956; **5G** — the fifth generation of mobile networks.
- **Optical fibre** carries data as light and gives the fastest links.

**Communication in Nepal**

- **Gorkhapatra**, the oldest newspaper, began in 1958 BS (1901).
- **Radio Nepal** began on **20 Chaitra 2007 (2 April 1951)**. **Nepal Television** was set up in 2041 BS.
- The first computer in Nepal was an **IBM 1401**, brought for the census of **2028 BS** (1971).
- Telephone, mobile and internet dates, and the latest density figures, are in Chapter 5 (topic 5.5). At the end of 2082/83, mobile broadband reached 95.09 percent of the population and fixed broadband 53.55 percent 🔄.

**Law, policy and institutions in Nepal**

- **Electronic Transactions Act, 2063** — still the main cyber law. It recognises electronic records and digital signatures and punishes computer offences. Cases go to the Information Technology Tribunal.
- **Individual Privacy Act, 2075.** **Right to Information Act, 2064.**
- **National Cyber Security Policy, 2080.** **Digital Nepal Framework, 2076** (Chapter 4).
- 🔄 **National Artificial Intelligence Policy, 2082** — approved by the Council of Ministers in Shrawan 2082 (August 2025). It provides for an **AI Regulation Council** and a **National AI Centre**; the Centre was opened on 24 Kartik 2082 (10 November 2025). The policy aims to prepare 5,000 skilled workers in AI.
- 🔄 **Not yet law:** a Bill on information technology and cyber security has been drafted several times since 2018, and a separate law on personal-data protection is being drafted. Neither had become law when this chapter was checked.
- 🔄 **Institutions:** the Ministry of Information and Communication; the Department of Information Technology, now under the Office of the Prime Minister and Council of Ministers; the Nepal Telecommunications Authority (the regulator); the Office of the Controller of Certification (digital signatures); the **Cyber Bureau of Nepal Police**, set up by a decision of **27 Jestha 2075 (10 June 2018)**.

**Cybercrime in Nepal** 🔄

- Nepal Police recorded **20,526 cybercrime complaints** in fiscal year **2082/83**. The figures of the two years before were 18,926 (2081/82) and 19,730 (2080/81).
- By platform: Facebook and Messenger **7,123**; TikTok 6,776; WhatsApp 3,105; Telegram 1,031; Instagram 628.
- Only 161 court cases were filed, and 155 persons were arrested.
- Common forms: fake accounts and insults; blackmail with private photos; fraud through fake jobs, lotteries, gifts and parcels; theft of money from mobile wallets and bank accounts; hacking of accounts and websites.
- In Bhadra 2082 the government blocked many social-media platforms that had not registered in Nepal. The Gen Z movement followed, and the block was lifted (Chapter 2).

**Kinds of cyber threat**

- **Hacking** — entering a system without permission.
- **Phishing** — a fake message or website that tricks a person into giving a password or bank detail.
- **Malware** — harmful software; a **virus** spreads from file to file; **ransomware** locks the files and demands money.
- **Identity theft** — using another person's name and details.
- **Cyber bullying** — insulting or threatening a person online.
- **Denial-of-service attack** — flooding a website with traffic so that real users cannot reach it.

**Rules of safety**

- Use long, different passwords and **two-step login**.
- Do not open unknown links or attachments. Check that a payment page begins with "https".
- Never tell anyone the one-time password (OTP) sent to your phone.
- Keep software updated; use antivirus software and a **firewall**; keep a second copy (backup) of important files.
- Think before you post. Report a crime to the Cyber Bureau or the nearest police office.

## 7.6 Biotechnology, alternative energy and space (जैविक प्रविधि, वैकल्पिक ऊर्जा र अन्तरिक्ष)

#### 1 · Why it matters

"Biotechnology, information and communication technology and alternative energy" is one line of the syllabus in every province that could be read. Space is not named in that line, but the current-affairs unit asks for "scientific" events, and satellites and Moon missions are the usual choice. This topic holds three 🔄 facts of 2026: Artemis II, the Munal satellite and the science Nobel Prizes.

#### 2 · Simple explanation

**Biotechnology** (जैविक प्रविधि) is the use of living things — bacteria, yeast, plant and animal cells — or their parts to make useful products. Making curd is old biotechnology. Moving a gene from one living thing to another is modern biotechnology.

**Alternative energy** (वैकल्पिक ऊर्जा) means sources other than the usual ones. In Nepal the word covers small water power, the sun, wind, biogas and better stoves — clean, local sources that replace firewood, kerosene and imported fuel.

**Space technology** puts satellites round the earth for communication, weather forecasting, maps and research.

#### 3 · Key facts

**Biotechnology — old and new**

- **Fermentation** by tiny living things gives curd (*Lactobacillus* bacteria), bread and alcohol (yeast), cheese, and Nepali foods such as gundruk and sinki.
- **Genetic engineering** moves a chosen gene into another living thing. The result is a **genetically modified organism (GMO)**.
  - **Human insulin** for diabetics is made by bacteria that carry the human gene; it went on sale in 1982.
  - **Bt cotton** carries a gene from a soil bacterium and resists the bollworm. **Golden rice** carries genes that give vitamin A.
- **Tissue culture** grows whole plants from tiny pieces in the laboratory. It gives disease-free seed potato, banana and orchid plants.
- **Cloning** makes an exact genetic copy. **Dolly the sheep**, born in Scotland in **1996**, was the first mammal cloned from an adult body cell.
- **Test-tube baby** (in-vitro fertilisation): the egg is fertilised outside the body. The first, **Louise Brown**, was born in England on **25 July 1978**. Nepal's first test-tube baby was born in 2061 BS (2005).
- **Vaccines and medicines:** the hepatitis B vaccine and the new messenger-RNA vaccines against COVID-19 are products of biotechnology.
- **Gene editing** (the CRISPR method) cuts and changes DNA at a chosen place.
- **Farming and environment:** biofertilisers, plant-based pesticides, biogas, fuel alcohol from sugarcane, and microbes that clean oil and waste.
- **Risks:** the safety of modified food is debated; local seed may be lost; cloning and gene editing of humans raise questions of ethics.
- **Nepal:** the National Biotechnology Policy is of 2063 BS. The **Nepal Academy of Science and Technology**, set up in **2039 BS** (1982) at Khumaltar, Lalitpur, and the Nepal Agricultural Research Council do the main research.
- 🔄 A separate **Ministry of Science, Technology and Innovation** was created in Baisakh 2083 (May 2026).
- **National Science Day** in Nepal is **1 Ashoj**, marked since 2070 BS. The world's Science Day for Peace and Development is **10 November**.

**Energy — the basic words**

- **Renewable** sources do not run out: sun, wind, water, biomass, heat from inside the earth.
- **Non-renewable** sources will run out: coal, petroleum and natural gas — the **fossil fuels**.
- Nepal has no petroleum production of its own; every litre is imported. About half of all households still cook mainly on firewood (51 percent in the Census of 2078; see Chapter 6).

**Alternative energy in Nepal**

- **Size classes of water power:** micro — up to 100 kilowatts; mini — 100 kilowatts to 1 megawatt; small — 1 to 25 megawatts. Large hydropower is in Chapter 1 (topic 1.8).
- **Biogas** (गोबर ग्यास) forms when dung and other waste rot without air. Its main gas is **methane**. The left-over slurry is good manure. Nepal's first biogas plant was built in 1955.
- **Solar energy:** photovoltaic panels turn light into electricity; solar heaters warm water; solar dryers and cookers use the heat directly.
- **Wind energy** is used in a few windy places, often together with solar panels in small local grids.
- **Improved cooking stoves** and electric stoves cut smoke and firewood.
- The **Alternative Energy Promotion Centre** (वैकल्पिक ऊर्जा प्रवर्द्धन केन्द्र) was set up on **18 Kartik 2053 (3 November 1996)**.
- 🔄 **Its results up to late 2025:** about **457,000 household biogas plants**; **40.7 megawatts** of micro and mini hydropower; about 49 megawatts of solar systems; more than 1.6 million improved cooking stoves. Its projects have earned about USD 36 million from the sale of carbon credits (topic 7.9).
- 🔄 **Solar on the national grid:** about 140 megawatts of solar plants were working in 2025. In fiscal year 2082/83 the Nepal Electricity Authority signed power-purchase agreements for 49 solar projects of 675 megawatts in all, against only five water-power projects of 28.6 megawatts.
- 🔄 **Targets:** 28,500 megawatts of clean power by 2035 (Chapter 1 and the climate pledge in topic 7.8).
- A Green Hydrogen Policy was approved in 2080 BS.
- **Why Nepal needs alternative energy:** scattered villages are costly to reach by the grid; firewood smoke harms health and forests; petroleum drains foreign exchange; clean energy cuts greenhouse gases.

**Space**

- **Sputnik 1**, launched by the Soviet Union on **4 October 1957**, was the first artificial satellite.
- **Yuri Gagarin** was the first human in space (12 April 1961). **Valentina Tereshkova** was the first woman (1963). **Neil Armstrong** was the first to walk on the Moon (20 July 1969, Apollo 11).
- A **geostationary satellite** circles about 36,000 km above the equator once in 24 hours, so it seems to stand still. Communication and weather satellites use this orbit.
- **Navigation systems:** GPS (United States), GLONASS (Russia), Galileo (European Union), BeiDou (China), NavIC (India).
- Space agencies: NASA (United States), ISRO (India), CNSA (China), ESA (Europe), JAXA (Japan), Roscosmos (Russia).
- The **James Webb Space Telescope** was launched on 25 December 2021.
- **Chandrayaan-3** of India landed near the south pole of the Moon on **23 August 2023** — the first craft to land in that region.
- 🔄 **Artemis II** of NASA carried **four astronauts round the Moon** and back from **1 to 10 April 2026**: Reid Wiseman, Victor Glover, Christina Koch and Jeremy Hansen of Canada. They were the first humans to travel beyond low earth orbit since Apollo 17 in 1972. They did not land.

#### 10 · Short-answer question

**Question.** What is alternative energy? Why does Nepal need it, and what has been done to promote it?

**Model answer.** **Alternative energy** means clean, renewable sources used in place of traditional fuel and fossil fuel — micro and mini hydropower, solar energy, wind, biogas and improved stoves. **Why Nepal needs it:** (1) many villages are far from the national grid, and small local plants reach them at lower cost; (2) about half of the households still cook on firewood, which harms the health of women and children and thins the forests; (3) all petroleum and cooking gas is imported, which drains foreign exchange; (4) clean energy cuts greenhouse gases and helps the pledge of net-zero emissions by 2045; (5) sun and falling water are plentiful. **What has been done:** the Alternative Energy Promotion Centre was set up on 18 Kartik 2053 (1996); subsidies are given for biogas, solar panels, micro-hydropower and stoves; by late 2025 about 457,000 household biogas plants, 40.7 megawatts of micro and mini hydropower and 1.6 million improved stoves had been installed; large solar plants now sell power to the grid; carbon credits from these projects have earned about USD 36 million; a Green Hydrogen Policy has been approved. **Problems:** high first cost, weak repair services, micro-hydro plants left idle when the grid arrives, and dependence on subsidy. **Way forward:** join small plants to the grid, promote electric cooking and electric vehicles, give easy loans, and train local technicians.

## 7.7 Ecosystem, natural cycles and biodiversity (पारिस्थितिक प्रणाली, जैव-भू-रासायनिक चक्र र जैविक विविधता)

#### 1 · Why it matters

The environment unit opens with "introduction and kinds of ecosystem, and the biogeochemical cycle", and goes on to "biodiversity, rare animals, birds and plants". Chapter 1 (topic 1.6) listed the parks. This topic gives the science behind them, the protected species, and the new tiger count.

#### 2 · Simple explanation

An **ecosystem** (पारिस्थितिक प्रणाली) is a community of living things together with the non-living things around them — air, water, soil, sunlight — all acting on one another. A pond is an ecosystem; so is a forest.

Energy enters an ecosystem from the sun and passes from plants to animals along a **food chain**. Materials such as water, carbon and nitrogen go round and round between living things and the earth; these rounds are the **biogeochemical cycles** (जैव-भू-रासायनिक चक्र).

**Biodiversity** (जैविक विविधता) is the variety of life — of genes, of species and of ecosystems.

#### 3 · Key facts

**Ecosystem**

- The word **ecology** was first used by Ernst Haeckel (1866). The word **ecosystem** was first used by **A.G. Tansley** (1935).
- **Non-living parts:** sunlight, air, water, soil, temperature.
- **Living parts:**
  - **producers** — green plants, which make food;
  - **consumers** — animals: plant-eaters (first-level), flesh-eaters (second and third level), and those that eat both;
  - **decomposers** — bacteria and fungi, which break down dead bodies and return the nutrients to the soil.
- **Kinds:** natural ecosystems are of land (forest, grassland, desert, mountain) or of water (pond, lake, river, wetland, sea). Artificial ecosystems are made by people (crop field, garden, fish pond, aquarium).

**Food chain and energy**

- A **food chain** shows who eats whom: grass → grasshopper → frog → snake → eagle. Many chains joined together make a **food web**.
- Green plants make food by **photosynthesis**: with sunlight and the green matter chlorophyll, they turn carbon dioxide and water into sugar and give out oxygen.
- Energy flows **one way** and is lost as heat at each step. Only about **10 percent** passes to the next level — the "ten percent law".
- Poisons that do not break down, such as DDT, grow stronger at each step of the chain. This is **biological magnification**.

**Biogeochemical cycles**

- **Water cycle:** evaporation from seas and land, and from leaves; condensation into clouds; rain and snow; flow back to the sea.
- **Carbon cycle:** plants take carbon dioxide from the air; animals, decay and burning return it.
- **Oxygen cycle:** plants give out oxygen; animals and fires use it.
- **Nitrogen cycle:** nitrogen is 78 percent of the air, but plants cannot use it as a gas. **Nitrogen-fixing bacteria** — such as *Rhizobium* in the root nodules of peas and beans — and lightning change it into forms that plants can take. Other bacteria return it to the air.
- **Phosphorus cycle:** phosphorus moves between rock, soil, water and living things; it has no gas stage.

**Biodiversity**

- **Three levels:** genetic diversity, species diversity, ecosystem diversity.
- **Conservation in place** (in-situ): national parks, reserves, conservation areas, community forests. **Conservation outside the natural home** (ex-situ): zoos, botanical gardens, seed banks and gene banks.
- **Threats:** loss of habitat; poaching and illegal trade; **invasive foreign plants** such as mikania ("mile-a-minute"), water hyacinth, lantana and parthenium; pollution; forest fire; climate change; conflict between people and wild animals.
- Nepal's figures — 118 ecosystems, 35 forest types, 20 protected areas — are in Chapter 1.

**Protected and rare animals of Nepal**

- The **National Parks and Wildlife Conservation Act, 2029** gives full protection to **26 mammals, 9 birds and 3 reptiles**.
- **Protected mammals** include the tiger, snow leopard, clouded leopard, one-horned rhinoceros, wild elephant, red panda, musk deer, swamp deer, blackbuck, wild water buffalo (arna), gaur, wild yak, river dolphin, pangolin, hispid hare and pygmy hog.
- **The 9 protected birds:** danphe (Impeyan pheasant), monal (crimson-horned pheasant), cheer pheasant, Bengal florican, lesser florican, sarus crane, giant hornbill, black stork, white stork.
- **The 3 protected reptiles:** gharial crocodile, python, golden monitor lizard.
- The **Spiny Babbler** (काँडे भ्याकुर) is the only bird found nowhere but in Nepal.
- **Vultures** fell sharply because the cattle medicine diclofenac poisoned them; Nepal banned it in 2006 and runs "vulture restaurants" that serve safe carcasses.
- Breeding centres: gharial at Kasara, Chitwan; elephant at Khorsor, Chitwan.

**Latest counts** 🔄

- **Tiger: 429** — National Tiger and Prey Base Survey, announced on **13 Shrawan 2083 (29 July 2026)**, International Tiger Day. The count of 2022 was 355; that of 2009 was 121.
- By area: **Chitwan 145; Bardiya 112; Parsa 71; Banke 51; Shuklaphanta 50.** Bardiya was the only area where the number fell (from 125).
- **One-horned rhinoceros: 752** (count of 2021), of which 694 in Chitwan. One report says the next count has been put off to 2027.
- **Snow leopard: 397** (first national estimate, 2025).

**Rare and valuable plants**

- **Yarsagumba** — a fungus that grows on a caterpillar in high pastures; a costly medicine.
- **Panchaunle**, **kutki**, **jatamasi** and **chiraito** — medicinal herbs of the mountains.
- **Lauth salla** (Himalayan yew) — its leaves give a medicine against cancer.
- The government bans or controls the collection of panchaunle and the bark of okhar (walnut), and the felling of trees such as champ, khayar, sal, simal, satisal and bijaysal.

**World bodies and agreements**

- **IUCN** (International Union for Conservation of Nature, 1948) keeps the **Red List** of threatened species.
- **CITES** (1973) controls trade in endangered species; Nepal has been a party since 1975.
- **Convention on Biological Diversity** (Rio, 1992).
- **Ramsar Convention** on wetlands (1971) — Nepal's ten sites are in Chapter 1.
- **Kunming–Montreal Global Biodiversity Framework** (2022): protect 30 percent of the land and sea by 2030 — "30 by 30".

#### 10 · Short-answer question

**Question.** What is an ecosystem? Describe its parts and explain how energy flows through it.

**Model answer.** An **ecosystem** is a unit of nature in which living things and their non-living surroundings act on one another and exchange energy and materials. A pond, a forest and a paddy field are ecosystems. **Parts:** (1) **non-living** — sunlight, air, water, soil and temperature; (2) **living** — (a) **producers**, the green plants that make food by photosynthesis; (b) **consumers**, the animals — plant-eaters at the first level, flesh-eaters at the second and third levels; (c) **decomposers**, the bacteria and fungi that break down dead plants and animals and return nutrients to the soil. **Flow of energy:** the sun is the source. Plants trap a small part of its light as food. Plant-eaters take this food, and flesh-eaters take it from them, along a **food chain** such as grass → grasshopper → frog → snake → eagle. At every step most of the energy is used up or lost as heat; only about **10 percent** reaches the next level. So energy flows in one direction and is never recycled, and a chain seldom has more than four or five links. **Materials**, on the other hand, go round in cycles — the water, carbon, oxygen and nitrogen cycles. **Importance:** if one part is damaged — forests cut, a top animal hunted out, poison added — the whole system is disturbed. Protecting ecosystems therefore protects people as well.

## 7.8 Pollution, greenhouse effect, climate change and the ozone layer (प्रदूषण, हरितगृह प्रभाव, जलवायु परिवर्तन र ओजोन तह)

#### 1 · Why it matters

The environment unit names "global warming, climate change, greenhouse effect, ozone depletion, environment, pollution" in one line. Bagmati adds waste management, deforestation and soil erosion. The roadmap marked this topic for fresh research, because the climate conferences and Nepal's pledges change every year. COP31 was in session while this chapter was being written.

#### 2 · Simple explanation

**Pollution** (प्रदूषण) is the adding of harmful things to air, water or soil, or of harmful noise, in amounts that hurt life.

The **greenhouse effect** (हरितगृह प्रभाव) is natural. Some gases in the air let the sun's light in but hold back part of the heat that the earth sends out, as the glass of a greenhouse does. Without them the earth would be frozen. People have added so much of these gases that the earth is growing warmer — **global warming** (विश्वव्यापी उष्णता). The lasting change in temperature, rain and seasons that follows is **climate change** (जलवायु परिवर्तन).

The **ozone layer** (ओजोन तह) is a different matter. It is a layer of ozone gas high in the air that shields life from the sun's harmful ultraviolet rays. Certain man-made chemicals thin it.

#### 3 · Key facts

**Pollution**

- **Air pollution:** smoke and fine dust from vehicles, brick kilns, factories, construction, burning of waste and stubble, forest fires and cooking fires; also haze that crosses borders. The most harmful part is very fine dust called **PM2.5**.
- **Water pollution:** sewage, factory waste, farm chemicals, plastic.
- **Soil (land) pollution:** plastic, chemicals, solid waste.
- **Noise pollution:** measured in **decibels**; long exposure above about 85 decibels harms hearing.
- **Acid rain** forms when sulphur dioxide and nitrogen oxides from burning fuel dissolve in rain.
- **Air Quality Index (AQI):** 0–50 good; 51–100 moderate; 101–150 unhealthy for sensitive groups; 151–200 unhealthy; 201–300 very unhealthy; above 300 hazardous.
- 🔄 **Kathmandu** is often among the most polluted cities of the world in the dry months before the monsoon. On 10 Baisakh 2083 (23 April 2026) it ranked second in the world, with an index of 247. Its average PM2.5 level for 2024 was about nine times the World Health Organization's guideline. The health ministry links about 42,000 deaths a year in Nepal to air pollution.
- **Disasters of pollution:** Minamata, Japan — mercury poisoning; Bhopal, India, 1984 — gas leak; Chernobyl, 26 April 1986 — nuclear accident.

**Waste, forests and soil** (Bagmati names these)

- **Solid-waste management:** separate rotting waste from the rest at home; compost the first; reuse and recycle the second; bury only what is left in a proper landfill. The rule is the **three Rs — reduce, reuse, recycle**.
- The **Solid Waste Management Act, 2068** makes the local level responsible. The waste of the Kathmandu Valley goes to the landfill at Banchare Danda, Nuwakot.
- **Deforestation** (वन विनाश): causes — clearing for farms and settlement, firewood and timber, roads, forest fire, overgrazing. Effects — landslides and floods, drying springs, loss of wildlife, more carbon dioxide in the air.
- **Soil erosion** (भूक्षय): the washing or blowing away of the top soil. Remedies — terraces, tree planting, grass strips, check dams, protection of the Chure hills.
- Forest and other wooded land cover 46.08 percent of Nepal (Chapter 1).

**Greenhouse gases**

- The main ones: **carbon dioxide** (burning coal, petroleum and wood), **methane** (paddy fields, cattle, rotting waste), **nitrous oxide** (fertiliser), and man-made **fluorinated gases**. Water vapour also holds heat.
- The Kyoto Protocol listed six: carbon dioxide, methane, nitrous oxide, hydrofluorocarbons, perfluorocarbons and sulphur hexafluoride.
- Carbon dioxide adds the most warming because there is so much of it. Molecule for molecule, methane is far stronger.
- **Nitrogen and oxygen**, which make up 99 percent of the air, are **not** greenhouse gases.

**Global warming — the record** 🔄

- **2024 is the warmest year on record** — about 1.55 °C above the average of 1850–1900, and the first calendar year above 1.5 °C.
- On 9 November 2026 the World Meteorological Organization said that **2023, 2024, 2025 and 2026 are the four warmest years** ever measured, and that the first nine months of 2026 were about 1.47 °C above the old average. A strong El Niño was building.
- **El Niño** is a natural warming of the surface of the Pacific Ocean near the equator. It raises world temperature for a time and often weakens the monsoon.

**Effects of climate change on Nepal**

- Glaciers shrink; glacial lakes grow and may burst (Chapter 1).
- The monsoon becomes irregular: long dry spells, then very heavy rain; more floods, landslides and droughts.
- Springs dry up in the hills; the snow line and the crop belts move higher.
- Forest fires increase. Mosquito-borne diseases such as dengue reach the hills.
- Nepal emits only a tiny share of the world's greenhouse gases, but it is among the countries most at risk. This is the base of its demand for **climate justice**.

**Three words of climate action**

- **Mitigation** (न्यूनीकरण) — cutting the gases: clean energy, electric transport, forests.
- **Adaptation** (अनुकूलन) — adjusting to the change: early warning, stronger roads and houses, new seed, saving water.
- **Loss and damage** (हानि–नोक्सानी) — harm that can no longer be avoided or adapted to, for which vulnerable countries ask payment.

**World agreements on climate**

- **Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change (IPCC)** — the scientific body, set up in **1988**.
- **United Nations Framework Convention on Climate Change (UNFCCC)** — signed at the Earth Summit in Rio de Janeiro, **1992**. Its yearly meeting is the **Conference of the Parties (COP)**.
- **Kyoto Protocol** — **1997**; in force from 2005; fixed cuts for rich countries.
- **Paris Agreement** — adopted on **12 December 2015**; in force from 4 November 2016. It aims to hold warming **well below 2 °C** and to try for **1.5 °C**. Each country files its own pledge, called a **Nationally Determined Contribution (NDC)**. Nepal ratified the Agreement in 2016.

**Nepal and climate change** 🔄

- **Cabinet at Kalapatthar:** on 19 Mangsir 2066 (4 December 2009) the Council of Ministers met near the Sagarmatha base camp to draw the world's eyes to the melting Himalaya.
- **Policies:** Climate Change Policy, 2076; National Adaptation Plan, 2021–2050; long-term strategy for **net-zero emissions by 2045**.
- **Third NDC** (sent to the United Nations in May 2025): cut net greenhouse-gas emissions by **17.12 percent by 2030** and **26.79 percent by 2035**, measured against "business as usual". Its cost is put at USD 73.74 billion, of which Nepal expects about 85 percent from abroad. It repeats the target of 28,500 megawatts of clean power by 2035.
- **Sagarmatha Sambaad** (सगरमाथा संवाद): the first was held in Kathmandu on **2–4 Jestha 2082 (16–18 May 2025)** on the theme "Climate Change, Mountains and the Future of Humanity". It ended with a **25-point "Sagarmatha Call for Action"**. The second is due in 2027.
- **At COP31** Nepal's delegation is led in the first week by Foreign Minister Shisir Khanal and in the second by Gita Chaudhary, Minister for Agriculture, Forests and Environment. Nepal has put forward the Bhote Koshi flood as proof of climate harm, asks for easy access to the loss-and-damage fund, and — with Bhutan — has raised the subject "1.5 °C and mountain regions". The conference had not ended when this chapter was checked.
- **Law:** the **Environment Protection Act, 2076** and its Rules of 2077 require a study before a project begins. There are three levels: **brief environmental study**, **initial environmental examination (IEE)** and **environmental impact assessment (EIA)**, the fullest. Article 30 of the Constitution gives the right to a clean and healthy environment and to compensation from the polluter.

**The ozone layer**

- Ozone is a gas whose molecule has **three atoms of oxygen**.
- The ozone layer lies in the **stratosphere**, roughly 15 to 35 km above the ground. It absorbs most of the sun's harmful **ultraviolet** rays.
- It is thinned by **chlorofluorocarbons (CFCs)** — once used in refrigerators, air conditioners, spray cans and foam — and by a few other chemicals. The thinning over Antarctica is called the **ozone hole**; it was reported in 1985.
- More ultraviolet light means more skin cancer and cataract, weaker defence against disease, and damage to crops and sea life.
- **Vienna Convention**, 1985. **Montreal Protocol**, signed on **16 September 1987**, to stop the use of ozone-harming chemicals. **16 September** is the International Day for the Preservation of the Ozone Layer. The **Kigali Amendment** of 2016 cuts hydrofluorocarbons, which replaced CFCs but warm the earth.
- The layer is slowly healing. The Montreal Protocol is counted as the most successful environmental treaty.
- Ozone near the ground, formed in smog, is a pollutant.

## 7.9 Sustainable development, the Sustainable Development Goals and carbon trade (दिगो विकास, दिगो विकास लक्ष्य र कार्बन व्यापार)

#### 1 · Why it matters

"Sustainable development" is the first word of the long environment line in the syllabus. Bagmati has a separate sub-unit on the Sustainable Development Goals and names "carbon trade"; Karnali names both; Koshi names the Goals in its economy unit. Carbon trade became a live subject in Nepal in 2082, with a new set of rules and the first payment.

#### 2 · Simple explanation

**Sustainable development** (दिगो विकास) is development that meets the needs of people today without spoiling the chance of people in the future to meet theirs. It stands on three legs: the **economy**, **society** and the **environment**. Cutting a whole forest for quick money is development, but it is not sustainable.

**Carbon trade** (कार्बन व्यापार) is the buying and selling of the right to emit greenhouse gases. A country or company that cuts or soaks up emissions earns **carbon credits**. It can sell them to another that needs to balance its own emissions. A forest that is kept standing can thus earn money.

#### 3 · Key facts

**Sustainable development**

- The best-known definition comes from the report ***Our Common Future*** (1987) of the World Commission on Environment and Development, headed by Gro Harlem Brundtland. It is called the **Brundtland Report**.
- **Three pillars:** economic growth, social justice, protection of the environment.
- **Guiding ideas:**
  - fairness between generations;
  - **polluter pays** — the one who pollutes bears the cost;
  - **precaution** — where serious harm is possible, act even before the proof is complete;
  - study of the effect on the environment before a project is approved.
- **Road to the idea:**
  - **1972** — United Nations Conference on the Human Environment, **Stockholm**; the United Nations Environment Programme was set up, with its seat at **Nairobi**.
  - **1987** — Brundtland Report.
  - **1992** — **Earth Summit**, Rio de Janeiro: Agenda 21, and the conventions on climate change and on biological diversity.
  - **2000** — eight Millennium Development Goals, to 2015.
  - **2002** — World Summit on Sustainable Development, Johannesburg.
  - **2012** — "Rio+20" conference; its paper was *The Future We Want*.
  - **2015** — the **Sustainable Development Goals**.

**The Sustainable Development Goals** (the full list is in Chapter 5, topic 5.10)

- Adopted by the United Nations on 25 September 2015; **17 goals and 169 targets**; to be reached by **2030**; motto "leave no one behind".
- They are often grouped under **five Ps: People, Planet, Prosperity, Peace, Partnership**.
- **Goals that belong to this chapter:** Goal 3 — good health and well-being; Goal 6 — clean water and sanitation; Goal 7 — affordable and clean energy; Goal 11 — sustainable cities and communities; Goal 12 — responsible consumption and production; Goal 13 — climate action; Goal 14 — life below water; Goal 15 — life on land.
- In Nepal the **National Planning Commission** guides and reports on the Goals; they are built into the periodic plans and the budget. 🔄 Nepal stood 90th of 167 countries on the SDG Index of 2026 (Chapter 5).

**Carbon trade — the ideas**

- **One carbon credit = one tonne of carbon dioxide** (or the same warming effect from another gas) cut or removed.
- The **Kyoto Protocol** (1997) created three "flexible mechanisms":
  - **Emissions Trading** — countries with targets trade spare allowances;
  - **Clean Development Mechanism (CDM)** — a rich country pays for a clean project in a developing country and counts the credits;
  - **Joint Implementation** — two countries with targets share a project.
- **Article 6 of the Paris Agreement** (2015) is the new base: 6.2 — trade between two countries by agreement; 6.4 — a market supervised by the United Nations; 6.8 — cooperation without a market.
- **Two kinds of market:** the **compliance** market, where the law sets a limit on emissions; and the **voluntary** market, where companies buy credits by choice.
- **REDD+** — "reducing emissions from deforestation and forest degradation", together with conserving and enlarging forests. It pays developing countries for keeping forests.
- **Carbon sink** — anything that takes in more carbon than it gives out: forests, soil, the sea. **Carbon footprint** — the total greenhouse gas caused by a person, a product or a country. **Net zero** — emissions and removals in balance. **Carbon tax** — a tax on fuel according to its carbon; it is a different tool from trading.

**Carbon trade in Nepal** 🔄

- **Alternative Energy Promotion Centre:** the first seller. Its biogas, micro-hydropower and stove projects, registered under the Clean Development Mechanism and similar schemes, have earned about **USD 36 million**.
- **Forest carbon:** Nepal signed an agreement with the World Bank's Forest Carbon Partnership Facility in February 2021 for the forests of thirteen districts of the Terai Arc Landscape. The **first payment — USD 9.4 million for about 1.88 million tonnes** of carbon dioxide — was announced on 28 Kartik 2082 (14 November 2025). The money is shared with forest communities under a benefit-sharing plan.
- **Carbon Trade Regulations, 2082** (कार्बन व्यापार नियमावली, २०८२): approved by the Council of Ministers on **15 Mangsir 2082 (1 December 2025)** under the Environment Protection Act, 2076. They let government bodies, **the private sector**, and provincial and local governments run projects and sell credits; provide for a **national carbon registry**; and open the way to trade under Article 6 of the Paris Agreement. Before these rules, in practice only the Centre's projects were traded.
- **At COP31** (November 2026) Nepal and the Republic of Korea planned to sign a memorandum of understanding on carbon trade.
- **Why it suits Nepal:** forest and other wooded land cover 46 percent of the country, most of it under community management; clean hydropower can replace dirtier fuel at home and abroad; emissions are low.
- **Cautions:** measuring carbon is costly and technical; prices swing; communities must receive a fair share; a credit sold abroad cannot also be counted towards Nepal's own pledge.

## 7.10 Population, urbanisation, migration and disaster management (जनसङ्ख्या, सहरीकरण, बसाइँसराइ र विपद् व्यवस्थापन)

#### 1 · Why it matters

The environment line of the syllabus ends with "population, urbanisation, settlement (migration)". Bagmati's unit is called "Population, environment and disaster management" and names earthquake, flood and landslide, storm, fire and road accidents, "their state, risks and the arrangements for managing them". Chapter 6 gave the census. This topic gives the movement of people and the management of disasters — including the Bhote Koshi flood of 2083.

#### 2 · Simple explanation

People and the environment press on each other. More people need more land, water, wood and energy. When villages cannot support them, people move — to the Terai, to towns, abroad. This movement is **migration** (बसाइँसराइ). The growth of towns is **urbanisation** (सहरीकरण).

A **hazard** is a danger — an earthquake, a flood. It becomes a **disaster** (विपद्) when it strikes people who are exposed and unprepared. **Disaster management** is everything done before, during and after to save lives and property.

#### 3 · Key facts

**Population and environment**

- World population 🔄: about **8.3 billion** in 2026. It passed 8 billion on 15 November 2022. India has been the most populous country since 2023.
- **World Population Day: 11 July** — the day in 1987 when the world reached five billion.
- **Thomas Malthus** (1798) warned that population grows faster than food.
- Nepal (Census of 2078; Chapter 6): 29,164,578 people; growth 0.92 percent a year; density 198 per square kilometre.
- The **fertility rate** in the census was **1.94** children per woman — below the **replacement level of 2.1**. Nepal's worry has turned from "too many births" to ageing, emptying hills and the loss of youth abroad.
- 🔄 **National Population Policy, 2082** — approved by the Council of Ministers on **23 Ashad 2082 (7 July 2025)**. It aims to hold fertility at the replacement level, manage migration, use the youthful population well and prepare for ageing. It replaces the policy of 2071.

**Urbanisation**

- Nepal has 293 municipalities (6 metropolitan cities, 11 sub-metropolitan cities, 276 municipalities). They hold **66.17 percent** of the people — but many of them contain villages and fields.
- A fairer measure, the **"degree of urbanisation"** report of the National Statistics Office (January 2024), classes the people of 2021 as **urban 27.07 percent, peri-urban 39.75 percent, rural 33.19 percent**. In 2011 the urban share was 22.31 percent.
- **Problems of fast, unplanned urban growth:** crowded and unsafe housing; settlements on river banks; traffic and air pollution; shortage of water; heaps of waste; loss of farmland; greater loss in earthquakes and fires.
- **Remedies:** land-use plans, building codes, public transport, open spaces, planned small towns, services in rural areas.

**Migration**

- **Internal migration** is movement inside the country; **international migration** crosses the border. **In-migration** and **out-migration** describe the same move seen from the two ends.
- **Push factors** drive people out: poverty, lack of work, land, schools and hospitals; disasters. **Pull factors** draw them in: jobs, services, safety, fertile land.
- Census of 2078: **8.3 million** people — **29.2 percent** of those born in Nepal — were living in a local level or district other than that of their birth.
- The largest stream is **from village to town** (51.3 percent of recent moves). **Bagmati** has the highest rate of in-migration (43.5 percent).
- The long stream **from hill and mountain to the Terai** has raised the Terai's share of the people from 48.4 percent (2058) to 50.27 percent (2068) and **53.61 percent** (2078). The population fell in 34 districts, mostly in the hills and mountains.
- **Abroad:** 2,190,592 persons were absent abroad at the census, 17.8 percent of them women. Remittance is in Chapter 5.
- **Effects:** money and new skills come home; but farms lie fallow, villages are left to the old, families are divided and trained youth are lost.

**Disaster — the basic ideas**

- **Risk = hazard × exposure × vulnerability.**
- **Natural disasters:** earthquake, flood, landslide, glacial-lake flood, storm, lightning, drought, cold wave, avalanche, forest fire. **Non-natural disasters:** fire, road and air accidents, epidemics, industrial accidents.
- **The cycle of disaster management:**
  - **before** — prevention and **mitigation** (reducing the risk), **preparedness** (plans, drills, early warning, stores);
  - **during** — **response** (search, rescue, relief);
  - **after** — **recovery** (rehabilitation and reconstruction — "build back better").

**Law and institutions**

- **Constitution:** disaster management is in Schedule 8 (powers of the local level) and Schedule 9 (powers shared by all three tiers). Article 51 lists early warning, preparedness, rescue, relief and rehabilitation among the policies of the State. Article 267 allows the Nepal Army to be used in disaster work.
- **Disaster Risk Reduction and Management Act, 2074** (विपद् जोखिम न्यूनीकरण तथा व्यवस्थापन ऐन). It replaced the Natural Calamity (Relief) Act, 2039, which dealt mainly with relief after the event. Its Rules are of 2076.
- **Bodies under the Act:**
  - **National Council** for Disaster Risk Reduction and Management — chaired by the **Prime Minister**; makes policy.
  - **Executive Committee** — chaired by the **Home Minister**; carries out the policy.
  - **National Disaster Risk Reduction and Management Authority** (राष्ट्रिय विपद् जोखिम न्यूनीकरण तथा व्यवस्थापन प्राधिकरण) — the working body and secretariat, in operation since 2076 BS, headed by a chief executive. 🔄 Dharma Raj Uprety held the post in 2083.
  - **Province Disaster Management Council** — chaired by the **Chief Minister**; and a provincial executive committee under the minister for internal affairs.
  - **District Disaster Management Committee** — chaired by the **Chief District Officer**.
  - **Local Disaster Management Committee** — chaired by the chairperson of the rural municipality or the mayor.
- The **National Emergency Operation Centre** of the Ministry of Home Affairs collects information and coordinates response. The Nepal Army, Nepal Police and the Armed Police Force do search and rescue. Relief money comes from disaster funds at each tier and from the Prime Minister's Disaster Relief Fund.
- **World frameworks:** the Hyogo Framework (2005–2015) was followed by the **Sendai Framework for Disaster Risk Reduction, 2015–2030**, adopted at Sendai, Japan, on 18 March 2015. It has **four priorities** and **seven targets**.
- **Days:** International Day for Disaster Risk Reduction — **13 October** (theme of 2026: "Resilience Starts at Home"). **Earthquake Safety Day in Nepal — 2 Magh**, the date of the great earthquake of 1990 BS.

**The hazards Bagmati names**

- **Earthquake:** Nepal lies where the Indian plate pushes under the Eurasian plate, so the whole country is at risk. Safety lies in following the **National Building Code**, in strong schools and hospitals, and in drills. On 23 Bhadra 2083 (8 September 2026) an earthquake of magnitude 5.3 centred on Lomanthang, Mustang, was felt widely; no loss was reported.
- **Flood and landslide:** the monsoon brings them every year — floods in the Terai, landslides and debris flows in the hills. Causes that people add: building on river banks and steep slopes, roads cut without engineering, quarrying, loss of forest.
- **Windstorm:** thunderstorms before the monsoon damage houses and crops. A violent windstorm struck Bara and Parsa on 17 Chaitra 2075 (31 March 2019).
- **Fire:** most common in the dry, windy months of Chaitra and Baisakh, above all in the thatched settlements of the Terai; forest fires peak in the same season.
- **Road accidents:** a daily disaster. Causes — careless and tired drivers, speed, drink, overloading, old vehicles, narrow and broken roads. Remedies — strict licensing and testing of vehicles, safer road design, crash barriers, time limits for drivers, quick ambulance service. The United Nations has named 2021–2030 the second Decade of Action for Road Safety, with the aim of halving road deaths.

**The Bhote Koshi flood of 2083** 🔄

- **When and where:** the morning of **10 Bhadra 2083 (26 August 2026)**; the Lhende stream and the Bhote Koshi near **Rasuwagadhi** on the Nepal–China border, then down the Trishuli and the Narayani.
- **Cause, as understood so far:** a large mass of ice and rock broke from a glacier above the Lhende valley; the debris dammed the stream, and the dam burst. The shaking was first recorded as an earthquake of magnitude 4.4; the United States Geological Survey later said it came from the collapse itself. Scientists were still studying the event.
- **Loss of life:** the disaster authority's figures of early November 2026 were **more than 1,450 dead** (1,457 in one notice, 1,464 in another) and about **5,285 missing**; more than 13,700 people were rescued. Many of the dead and missing were workers at hydropower sites, travellers, pilgrims and foreign tourists. Bodies were carried as far as Chitwan and Nawalparasi.
- **Damage:** the rapid assessment put the loss at about **Rs 408 billion** and the need for rebuilding at about Rs 723 billion. About 7,570 houses, 13 hydropower projects of 759 megawatts in all, the Rasuwagadhi customs point and the road from Betrawati to the border were damaged or destroyed.
- **Law:** an **ordinance to amend some Nepal Acts relating to disaster, 2083**, published in the Gazette on 23 Ashoj 2083 (9 October 2026), lets the Government declare the missing dead through a special procedure, without the long court process.
- **Relief announced:** Rs 200,000 to a family that lost one member, and Rs 100,000 more for each further member.
- It is the worst disaster in Nepal since the earthquake of 2072.

**Other recent events** 🔄

- **Monsoon of 2083:** it lasted 118 days. Apart from the Bhote Koshi flood, monsoon disasters killed 133 people, against 140 the year before.
- **Budhi Gandaki flood, 25 Ashoj 2083 (11 October 2026):** a landslide dammed a tributary in northern Gorkha and burst; some 35 to 45 houses, several bridges and a hydropower camp were swept away, and five people were missing. Warning messages were sent to mobile phones downstream.

#### 10 · Short-answer question

**Question.** Describe the legal and institutional arrangements for disaster management in Nepal. What more is needed?

**Model answer.** Nepal faces earthquakes, floods, landslides, glacial floods, fires, storms and road accidents; the Bhote Koshi flood of 10 Bhadra 2083 showed the scale of the risk. **Legal arrangements:** the Constitution places disaster management in Schedules 8 and 9 and names preparedness, rescue and relief among the policies of the State; the **Disaster Risk Reduction and Management Act, 2074** and its Rules of 2076 cover the whole cycle from risk reduction to reconstruction; the Local Government Operation Act gives duties to the local level; the National Building Code guides safe construction; an ordinance of 2083 speeds the legal recognition of missing persons. **Institutions:** the **National Council** under the Prime Minister for policy; the **Executive Committee** under the Home Minister for execution; the **National Disaster Risk Reduction and Management Authority** as the working body; the **Province Disaster Management Council** under the Chief Minister; the **District Disaster Management Committee** under the Chief District Officer; the **Local Disaster Management Committee** under the chairperson or mayor; the National Emergency Operation Centre; the Nepal Army, Nepal Police and Armed Police Force for search and rescue; disaster funds at each tier. Nepal also follows the **Sendai Framework, 2015–2030**. **What more is needed:** hazard maps and land-use plans that keep settlements and projects away from river beds and unstable slopes; watching of glaciers and lakes with shared data across the border; early warning that reaches every phone; trained and equipped local rescue teams; strict building codes and road-safety rules; insurance; and quick, honest relief and reconstruction.

### D. Five short-answer questions

**1. Distinguish between soap and detergent. What harm does plastic do, and how can it be reduced?**
*Points for the answer:* soap — sodium or potassium salt of a fatty acid, made from fat or oil by saponification, weak in hard water, breaks down in nature · detergent — usually from petroleum products, works in hard water, some kinds pollute water · plastic — a polymer; does not rot; chokes drains, soil and animals; poisonous smoke when burnt; microplastics in water and food · remedies — reduce, reuse, recycle; cloth and jute bags; separate waste at home; ban or charge for thin bags; recycling industry; public awareness.

**2. What is biotechnology? Describe its uses in health, farming and industry.**
*Points for the answer:* use of living things or their parts to make useful products · old: curd, bread, alcohol, gundruk; modern: genetic engineering, tissue culture, cloning, gene editing · health — insulin, vaccines, test-tube baby, DNA tests · farming — improved and disease-free plants, biofertilisers, plant-based pesticides · industry and environment — biogas, fuel alcohol, cleaning of waste · risks — safety of modified food, loss of local seed, ethics · Nepal — policy of 2063, Nepal Academy of Science and Technology, the new science ministry of 2083.

**3. What are the causes and effects of air pollution in the Kathmandu Valley? Suggest measures.**
*Points for the answer:* bowl shape of the valley traps smoke · causes — vehicles, brick kilns, construction dust, burning of waste, forest fires and smoke from outside in the dry season · effects — lung and heart disease, about 42,000 deaths a year in Nepal linked to polluted air, poor visibility, loss to tourism · measures — emission tests and removal of old vehicles, electric transport, cleaner kilns, dust control at building sites, ban on open burning, more monitoring stations and public warnings, green belts, enforcement of the Environment Protection Act, 2076.

**4. What is sustainable development? Why is it important for Nepal?**
*Points for the answer:* meeting present needs without harming the future — Brundtland Report, 1987 · three pillars: economy, society, environment · Nepal's reasons — fragile mountains; dependence on forests, water and farming; disasters made worse by careless building, as the floods of 2081 and 2083 showed; tourism rests on nature · tools — environmental study before projects, community forestry, clean energy, land-use plans, the Sustainable Development Goals in plans and budgets · the aim: growth that lasts and reaches everyone.

**5. What are the causes and effects of internal migration and rapid urban growth in Nepal?**
*Points for the answer:* 29.2 percent of the native-born live away from their place of birth; village-to-town and hill-to-Terai streams · push — little land and work, few services, disasters; pull — jobs, schools, hospitals, roads · effects on origin — fallow fields, ageing villages, falling population in 34 districts · effects on destination — crowded housing, river-bank settlements, pollution, pressure on water and transport; Terai share 53.61 percent · measures — services and jobs in rural areas, planned small towns, land-use and building rules, the National Population Policy, 2082.

### E. Common mistakes

1. Calling a **light year** a unit of time.
2. Saying sound travels fastest in **air**.
3. Calling **rayon** a fully synthetic fibre, or **nylon** a natural one.
4. Treating **Bakelite** as a thermoplastic.
5. Exchanging **Lamarck** and **Darwin**.
6. Saying the **mother** decides the sex of the child.
7. Listing **malaria** as a viral disease, or **tuberculosis** as one.
8. Saying Nepal has eliminated **measles**; it is **rubella**.
9. Mixing the three child-mortality figures (**21, 28, 33**) or the three malnutrition figures (**25, 8, 19**).
10. Treating the **internet** and the **World Wide Web** as the same thing.
11. Giving the tiger number as **355**; it is **429** since July 2026.
12. Saying that **ozone depletion** causes global warming.
13. Placing **COP30** in Antalya or **COP31** in Belém.
14. Giving Nepal's net-zero year as **2050**; it is **2045**.
15. Naming the **Home Minister** as chair of the National **Council** for disaster management; it is the **Prime Minister**.
16. Quoting the Bhote Koshi figures without their date, or confusing that flood with the Rasuwagadhi flood of **Ashad 2082**.
