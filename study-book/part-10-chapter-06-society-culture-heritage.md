## 6.1 Population and the Census of 2078 (जनसङ्ख्या र राष्ट्रिय जनगणना २०७८)

#### 1 · Why it matters

Census figures are the raw material of the "social and cultural condition" unit: the sex ratio, the size of a household, the share of old people. Bagmati also has a separate unit that begins with population. A figure from the census is easy to set and easy to mark.

#### 2 · Simple explanation

A **census** (जनगणना) counts every person and every household in the country on one fixed day. It also records sex, age, education, language, religion and caste or ethnic group. Nepal takes a census every ten years.

A few terms are needed:

- **population growth rate** (जनसङ्ख्या वृद्धिदर) — how fast the population rises in a year;
- **density** (जनघनत्व) — persons per square kilometre;
- **sex ratio** (लैङ्गिक अनुपात) — the number of **men for every 100 women**;
- **household** (परिवार) — people who live together and share one kitchen;
- **working-age population** — people aged 15 to 59.

When the working-age group is large and has work, a country can grow quickly. This chance is called the **demographic dividend** (जनसाङ्ख्यिक लाभांश).

#### 3 · Key facts

**History of the census**

- The first count was made in **1968 BS (1911)**.
- The census of **2009–2011 BS** is regarded as the first modern census.
- Since then a census has been taken every ten years. The census of **2078 BS** is the **twelfth**.
- Article 281 of the Constitution asks the Government to review the special rights of women and the Dalit community along with each ten-yearly census.

**The Census of 2078**

- Full name: National Population and Housing Census, 2078.
- Taken by the Central Bureau of Statistics, now the **National Statistics Office**.
- Field count: **25 Kartik to 9 Mangsir 2078** (11 to 25 November 2021). **Census day: 9 Mangsir 2078 (25 November 2021).**
- Slogan: "मेरो गणना, मेरो सहभागिता" — my census, my participation.
- Final results: **10 Chaitra 2079 (24 March 2023)**. The tables on caste, language and religion followed on 19 Jestha 2080 (2 June 2023).

**People**

- **Population: 29,164,578.** It was 26,494,504 in 2068, so 2,670,074 people were added in ten years.
- **Growth:** **0.92 percent** a year (1.35 percent in the ten years before).
- **Women:** 14,911,027 (51.13 percent). **Men:** 14,253,551 (48.87 percent).
- **Sex ratio:** **95.59** men for every 100 women (94.16 in 2068).
- **Density:** **198** persons per square kilometre.
- **Age:** 0–14 years **27.83 percent**; 15–59 years **61.96 percent**; 60 years and above **10.21 percent**.
- **Disability:** 2.2 percent of the people have some kind of disability.
- **Marriage:** the average age at first marriage is 19 years — 21 for men and 18 for women.
- **Abroad:** about 2.19 million people were recorded as absent from their homes and living abroad.

**Households**

- **6,666,937 households**; **4.37 persons** in each on average (4.88 in 2068).
- By region: mountains 4.33; hills 3.99; Terai 4.73.
- Largest average household: **Rautahat**, 5.94. Smallest: **Gorkha and Dolakha**, 3.49.
- A woman heads **31.55 percent** of households.
- In **23.8 percent** of households a woman owns the house, the land or both.
- 86 percent of households live in a house of their own.
- 92.2 percent use electricity for light; 51 percent cook mostly with firewood; 95.5 percent use a toilet.

**Where people live**

- **Terai 53.61 percent; hills 40.31 percent; mountains 6.08 percent** (Chapter 1).
- **Municipalities 66.17 percent; rural municipalities 33.83 percent.**
- Largest province by population: **Bagmati**, 20.97 percent. Smallest: **Karnali**, 5.79 percent.
- Most populous districts: Kathmandu, Morang, Rupandehi, Jhapa and Sunsari. The least populous is Manang.

#### 7 · Exam traps

- The **sex ratio** counts **men per 100 women**. A ratio of 95.59 means women are more.
- The final total is **29,164,578**. The figure 29,192,480 is the preliminary result.
- The census of 2078 is the **twelfth**, not the eleventh.
- **Census day** is 9 Mangsir 2078. The field count began on 25 Kartik.
- "66.17 percent in municipalities" does not mean that two thirds of Nepalis live in cities. Many municipalities contain villages and farmland.
- A **household** is not a **house**. Several households may share one house.
- **Bagmati** has the largest population, but only by about two thousand over Madhesh.

#### 10 · Short-answer question

**Question.** Describe the main features of Nepal's population as shown by the Census of 2078.

**Model answer.** The twelfth census, taken on 9 Mangsir 2078, counted **29,164,578** people. **(1) Slow growth:** the population grew by only 0.92 percent a year, against 1.35 percent in the decade before. **(2) More women than men:** women are 51.13 percent; there are 95.59 men for every 100 women, partly because many men work abroad. **(3) Smaller families:** there are 6,666,937 households with 4.37 members on average, down from 4.88. **(4) A young but ageing people:** 61.96 percent are of working age (15–59), 27.83 percent are children and 10.21 percent are 60 or older. **(5) Uneven spread:** the Terai holds 53.61 percent of the people on about 17 percent of the land; density is 198 per square kilometre; Bagmati has the most people and Karnali the fewest. **(6) Movement to towns:** 66.17 percent live in municipalities. **(7) Social change:** literacy has reached 76.2 percent, and a woman heads 31.55 percent of households. **Meaning:** Nepal has a large working-age group now, but it must give that group skills and jobs at home before the population grows old.

## 6.2 Caste and ethnic groups (जातजाति)

#### 1 · Why it matters

"How many castes and ethnic groups?", "which is the largest?", "which group is endangered?" — such questions appear in every social and cultural unit. Gandaki and Sudurpashchim also ask about the peoples of their own province.

#### 2 · Simple explanation

Nepal is a country of many peoples. Article 3 of the Constitution says that the nation is made up of all Nepalis, who are **multi-ethnic, multilingual, multi-religious and multicultural**.

Two words are used together:

- **caste** (जात) — a group within the Hindu social order, such as Chhetri, Brahman or Bishwakarma;
- **ethnic group** or **nationality** (जाति, जनजाति) — a people with its own language, customs and homeland, such as Magar, Tharu, Tamang or Limbu.

The census counts both in one list. Law and policy also use broader names: **Khas Arya**, **Adivasi Janajati** (indigenous nationalities), **Madhesi**, **Dalit**, **Tharu** and **Muslim**. Seats, jobs and scholarships are shared among these clusters under the principle of inclusion.

#### 3 · Key facts

**In the census**

- The Census of 2078 lists **142** castes and ethnic groups. The census of 2068 listed 125, so 17 names were added.
- **Largest groups:**
  - Chhetri — **16.45 percent** (4,796,995);
  - Brahman (Hill) — **11.29 percent** (3,292,373);
  - Magar — **6.9 percent** (2,013,498);
  - Tharu — **6.2 percent** (1,807,124);
  - Tamang — **5.62 percent**.
- Then: Bishwakarma 5.04, Musalman 4.86, Newar 4.6, Yadav 4.21 and Rai 2.2 percent.
- **Very small groups:** Kusunda 253 persons; Raute 566.

**Broad clusters**

- **Khas Arya:** the Constitution explains it as the Chhetri, Brahman, Thakuri and Sanyasi (Dashnami) communities (Article 84).
- **Indigenous nationalities** (आदिवासी जनजाति):
  - **59** groups are listed under the National Foundation for Development of Indigenous Nationalities Act, **2058**;
  - they are sorted into five classes: endangered (10), highly marginalised (12), marginalised (20), disadvantaged (15) and advanced (2);
  - the two "advanced" groups are the **Newar** and the **Thakali**;
  - the **Magar** are the largest indigenous nationality.
- **Dalit:** communities that suffered untouchability. The National Dalit Commission's list has 26 castes — hill Dalit such as Bishwakarma, Pariyar and Mijar, and Madhesi Dalit such as Chamar, Musahar and Paswan.
- **Constitutional commissions** for groups (Articles 252 to 264): Women, Dalit, Inclusion, Indigenous Nationalities, Madhesi, Tharu and Muslim Commissions (Chapter 3).

**Peoples to know**

- **Raute:** the last nomadic people; they move through the forests of Karnali and Sudurpashchim.
- **Kusunda:** a tiny group whose language is related to no other.
- **Chepang:** hills of Chitwan, Makwanpur and Dhading.
- **Tharu:** the largest indigenous people of the Terai, from east to west.
- **Newar:** the Kathmandu Valley. **Sherpa:** Solukhumbu. **Thakali:** Thak Khola in Mustang.
- **Gurung (Tamu):** hills of Gandaki. **Limbu and Rai:** the Kirat peoples of the eastern hills.
- **Dhimal, Meche, Rajbanshi and Santhal (Satar):** Jhapa and Morang.

**Customary institutions**

- **Guthi** (Newar): a trust that runs festivals, temples and funerals.
- **Badghar** or **Bhalmansa** (Tharu): the village headman chosen each year at Maghi.
- **Rodhi** (Gurung): an evening house of song for the young.
- **Dhikur** (Thakali): a rotating savings and credit group.
- **Parma:** exchange of farm labour between households.
- **Kipat** (Limbu): the old communal land system of the east.

#### 10 · Short-answer question

**Question.** Who are the indigenous nationalities of Nepal? How are they classified, and what has the State done for them?

**Model answer.** **Indigenous nationalities** (आदिवासी जनजाति) are communities that have their own mother tongue, traditional customs, a separate social structure and a written or unwritten history. The Act of 2058 lists **59** such groups. **Classification:** on the basis of their social and economic condition they are sorted into five classes — **endangered** (10, such as Kusunda, Raute and Raji), **highly marginalised** (12, such as Chepang and Majhi), **marginalised** (20, such as Tharu and Tamang), **disadvantaged** (15, such as Magar, Gurung, Rai and Limbu) and **advanced** (2 — Newar and Thakali). **Steps by the State:** (1) the Constitution describes the nation as multi-ethnic (Article 3) and gives the right to language and culture (Article 32) and to social justice (Article 42); (2) an Indigenous Nationalities Commission and a Tharu Commission are constitutional bodies; (3) a share of seats in the proportional election and of posts in the civil service is set aside; (4) a national foundation works for their development; (5) endangered groups receive a social security allowance; (6) provinces have begun to use indigenous languages in government work. **What remains:** the smallest groups, such as the Kusunda and Raute, still risk losing their language and way of life.

## 6.3 Languages and scripts (भाषा र लिपि)

#### 1 · Why it matters

The number of mother tongues, the order of the largest languages, the script of a language and the Articles on language are regular questions. The use of provincial languages in government work is also fresh current affairs: Koshi began it on the very day this chapter was checked.

#### 2 · Simple explanation

A **mother tongue** (मातृभाषा) is the language a child first learns at home. Nepal has more than a hundred of them.

The Constitution separates two ideas:

- **languages of the nation** (राष्ट्रभाषा) — **all** mother tongues spoken in Nepal (Article 6);
- **official language** (सरकारी कामकाजको भाषा) — the language of government work. It is **Nepali in the Devanagari script** (Article 7). A province may add one or more languages by its own law.

A **script** (लिपि) is the set of letters in which a language is written. One script can serve many languages — Devanagari is used for Nepali, Maithili, Tharu and others — and one language can have a script of its own.

Languages belong to **families**. Most Nepalis speak a language of the Indo-European family, but most of the languages by number belong to the Sino-Tibetan family.

#### 3 · Key facts

**In the Constitution**

- **Article 6:** all mother tongues spoken in Nepal are languages of the nation.
- **Article 7:** Nepali in Devanagari is the official language; a province may choose, by provincial law, one or more languages spoken by a majority of its people as additional official languages.
- **Article 31 (5):** every community may get education in its mother tongue.
- **Article 32:** the right to use one's language and to protect one's script and culture.
- **Article 287:** the **Language Commission** (भाषा आयोग). It was formed on 23 Bhadra 2073 (8 September 2016).

**In the Census of 2078**

- **124** mother tongues (123 in 2068).
- **Nepali:** **44.86 percent** (13,084,457 speakers).
- **Maithili:** **11.05 percent**. **Bhojpuri:** 6.24. **Tharu:** 5.88. **Tamang:** 4.88.
- Then: Bajjika 3.89, Avadhi 2.96, Nepal Bhasa 2.96, Magar Dhut 2.78 and Doteli 1.7 percent.

**Language families**

- **Indo-European (Indo-Aryan branch):** Nepali, Maithili, Bhojpuri, Tharu, Bajjika, Avadhi, Doteli — spoken by about four fifths of the people.
- **Sino-Tibetan (Tibeto-Burman branch):** Tamang, Nepal Bhasa, Magar, Limbu, Gurung, Sherpa and the Rai languages — the largest family by number of languages.
- **Austro-Asiatic:** Santhali.
- **Dravidian:** Kurux (Uranw, also called Jhangad).
- **Standing alone:** **Kusunda**, related to no other language and close to extinction.

**Nepali and its neighbours**

- Nepali grew from the **Khas** language of the Karnali region; the **Sinja** valley of Jumla is called its cradle. Older names are Khas Kura, Parbate and Gorkhali.
- **Maithili** is the second language of Nepal. Its great poet is **Vidyapati**. Its old script is **Mithilakshar** (Tirhuta).
- **Nepal Bhasa** has a long written tradition. Its scripts include **Ranjana** and **Prachalit**.

**Official languages of the provinces** 🔄

- On 21 Bhadra 2078 (6 September 2021) the Language Commission recommended **11 languages** for use in the provinces (table below).
- **Bagmati:** passed the Province Official Language Act, 2080. **Tamang and Nepal Bhasa** came into use on **24 Baisakh 2081 (6 May 2024)**. Bagmati keeps that day as a language day with a provincial holiday.
- **Koshi:** on **25 Kartik 2083 (11 November 2026)** the province brought **13 languages** besides Nepali into government use. They include Maithili, Limbu, Tharu, Tamang, Magar, Bantawa, Chamling, Urdu, Rajbanshi, Nepal Bhasa, Sherpa and Santhali. The provincial Act had been authenticated on 11 Kartik 2083 (28 October 2026).
- A news report of October 2026 said that Madhesh had still not made such a law. No such law was found for Gandaki, Lumbini, Karnali or Sudurpashchim either.

**Other facts**

- International Mother Language Day: 21 February.
- The Language Commission counts languages and recommends how to protect them; many small languages have very few speakers left.

## 6.4 Religions and festivals (धर्म र चाडपर्व)

#### 1 · Why it matters

The share of each religion, the meaning of "secular", and the festival of each community are asked in almost every paper. Festivals are also the easiest way for an examiner to bring in a candidate's own province.

#### 2 · Simple explanation

Nepal is a **secular** state (धर्मनिरपेक्ष). Article 4 explains the word: religious and cultural freedom, **including protection of the religion and culture handed down from ancient times**. Every person may follow and protect his or her religion (Article 26). No one may convert another person by force or inducement, or disturb the religion of others.

Most Nepalis are Hindu, but Hindu and Buddhist worship are closely mixed, and Kirat, Muslim, Christian and nature-worshipping communities live beside them. This living together is called **religious tolerance** (धार्मिक सहिष्णुता).

A **festival** (चाडपर्व) marks a season, a harvest, a god or an event. A **jatra** (जात्रा) is a street festival in which an image is carried or a chariot is pulled.

#### 3 · Key facts

**Religions in the Census of 2078**

- The census records **10 religions**.
- **Hindu 81.19 percent** (23,677,744 people).
- **Buddhist 8.21 percent. Islam 5.09 percent. Kirat 3.17 percent. Christian 1.76 percent.**
- Smaller: Prakriti (nature worship), Bon, Jain, Bahai and Sikh.
- Nepal was a Hindu kingdom until 2063 BS. The Interim Constitution of 2063 first declared it secular (Chapter 3).

**Great festivals**

- **Dashain** (Bada Dashain): the largest festival; **15 days** in Ashoj, from **Ghatasthapana** to the full moon. Phulpati is the seventh day; on the tenth day, **Vijaya Dashami**, elders give tika and jamara.
- **Tihar** (Yamapanchak): **5 days** in Kartik — crow, dog, cow and Laxmi Puja, Govardhan Puja, and **Bhai Tika**. The Newar keep **Mha Puja** and the new year of Nepal Sambat on the fourth day.
- **Chhath:** worship of the **Sun** on the sixth day of the bright half of Kartik; the great festival of the Terai and Mithila.
- **Teej:** women's festival in Bhadra, with fasting, songs and red dress.
- **Maghe Sankranti (1 Magh):** kept across the country; **Maghi** is the new year of the **Tharu**.
- **Lhosar** (new year of Himalayan peoples): **Tamu Lhosar** of the Gurung on 15 Poush; **Sonam Lhosar** of the Tamang in Magh; **Gyalpo Lhosar** of the Sherpa and Tibetans in Falgun.
- **Buddha Jayanti:** full moon of Baisakh — the birth, enlightenment and death of the Buddha.
- **Fagu Purnima (Holi):** full moon of Falgun; kept a day later in the Terai.
- **Ubhauli** (Baisakh full moon) and **Udhauli** (Mangsir full moon): festivals of the **Kirat**, with the **Sakela** dance.
- **Maha Shivaratri:** Falgun; a great fair at Pashupatinath.
- **Eid** and **Bakar Eid** (Muslim) and **Christmas** (Christian) are public holidays for those communities.

**Jatras of the Kathmandu Valley**

- **Indra Jatra** (Kathmandu): the chariot of the living goddess **Kumari** is pulled; tradition credits King Gunakamadeva.
- **Gai Jatra:** the day after Janai Purnima; tradition credits **Pratap Malla**, who began it to console his queen.
- **Rato Machhindranath Jatra** (Lalitpur): the longest chariot festival; it ends with **Bhoto Jatra**; tradition links it with King Narendradeva.
- **Bisket Jatra** (Bhaktapur): at the new year of Bikram Sambat.
- **Ghode Jatra:** horse parade at Tundikhel in Chaitra.

**Festivals of regions and communities**

- **Jitiya:** fast of Tharu and Madheshi women in Ashoj.
- **Sama Chakewa** and **Jur Sital:** Mithila.
- **Bibaha Panchami:** Janakpur, the wedding of Ram and Sita, in Mangsir.
- **Gaura Parva:** Sudurpashchim, in Bhadra, with the Deuda dance.
- **Bhume Parva:** the Kham Magar of Rukum and Rolpa, at the start of Ashad.
- **Chasok Tangnam:** harvest festival of the Limbu.
- **Mani Rimdu:** masked dances of the Sherpa at Tengboche. **Tiji:** Lo Manthang, Mustang.
- **Yomari Punhi:** Newar harvest festival on the full moon of Mangsir.

**Calendars**

- **Bikram Sambat** is the official calendar. **Nepal Sambat** began in 879 AD (Chapter 2). The **Hijri** calendar guides Muslim festivals.

- **Indra Jatra** is in Kathmandu, **Bisket** in Bhaktapur, **Rato Machhindranath** in Lalitpur.
- Dates of lunar festivals move every year. Learn the month, not a fixed date.

## 6.5 Art, architecture, music and dance (कला, वास्तुकला, सङ्गीत र नृत्य)

#### 1 · Why it matters

Questions match a building with its style, a dance with its community, or an instrument with its players. They are simple if the lists are learned, and they often carry a province's name.

#### 2 · Simple explanation

**Architecture** (वास्तुकला) is the art of building. Nepal's old temples and palaces follow a few clear styles:

- **pagoda** (तले शैली) — several roofs, one above another, each smaller than the one below, held by carved wooden struts;
- **shikhara** (शिखर शैली) — a tall tower of stone or brick that curves to a point;
- **stupa** or **chaitya** (स्तूप शैली) — a solid dome on a base, with a spire and the eyes of the Buddha;
- **dome** (गुम्बज शैली) — rounded roofs and arches, of the kind called Mughal;
- the **European** style of the Rana palaces — white walls and long rows of columns.

**Art** (कला) covers painting, sculpture, wood-carving and metalwork. **Folk music and dance** (लोक सङ्गीत र नृत्य) belong to a community and are handed down without written notes.

#### 3 · Key facts

**Buildings and their styles**

- **Pagoda:** Pashupatinath; Changu Narayan; **Nyatapola** of Bhaktapur — five roofs, built by Bhupatindra Malla in 1702; Kasthamandap, the hall that gave Kathmandu its name.
- **Araniko** carried Nepali craft to Tibet and China in the 13th century and built the White Stupa of Beijing (Chapter 2).
- **Shikhara:** **Krishna Mandir** of Patan — stone, built by Siddhi Narsingh Malla in 1637; Mahaboudha of Patan.
- **Stupa:** **Swayambhunath** and **Boudhanath**; the four Ashoka stupas of Patan.
- **Dome:** **Janaki Temple** of Janakpur, completed in 1967 BS and also called Naulakha Mandir.
- **European style:** **Singha Durbar** and other Rana palaces.
- **Dharahara:** the tower first built by Bhimsen Thapa fell in the earthquake of 12 Baisakh 2072 (25 April 2015). The rebuilt tower was opened to the public on 3 Ashoj 2081 (19 September 2024).

**Arts and crafts**

- **Paubha:** the traditional religious painting of the Newar.
- **Thangka:** the Tibetan Buddhist scroll painting.
- **Mithila painting:** made by Maithil women on walls and paper; Janakpur is its centre.
- **Wood-carving:** the windows of the Kathmandu Valley, such as the Peacock Window of Bhaktapur.
- **Metalwork:** images cast by the lost-wax method in Patan.
- **Stone sculpture:** the reclining Vishnu of Budhanilkantha, from the Lichhavi period.
- **Crafts:** Dhaka cloth (Palpa, Tehrathum); pottery (Thimi, Bhaktapur); khukuri (Bhojpur); pashmina; hand-made paper from lokta.

**Musical instruments**

- **Panche Baja:** five instruments played by the Damai at weddings — damaha, tyamko, sanai, jhyali and dholaki. With more instruments, such as the narsingha, it becomes the Naumati Baja.
- **Madal:** the hand drum of the hills.
- **Sarangi:** the fiddle of the **Gandarbha**, the travelling singers.
- **Damphu:** the round drum of the **Tamang**.
- **Dhimay:** the big drum of the **Newar**.
- **Chyabrung:** the long drum of the **Limbu**.
- **Murchunga** and **binayo:** small mouth instruments of the Kirat.
- **Hudko:** the small drum of the far west.

**Folk dances**

- **Dhan Nach** (Limbu) and **Chyabrung dance** (Limbu); **Sakela** (Rai).
- **Tamang Selo** (Tamang).
- **Ghatu** and **Sorathi** (Gurung); **Kaura** and **Maruni** (Magar and the western hills).
- **Lakhe** and **Charya** dances (Newar); **Kartik Nach** of Patan.
- **Jhijhiya** and **Jat-Jatin** (Mithila); **Sakhiya** and the stick dance (Tharu).
- **Deuda** (Karnali and Sudurpashchim); **Hudkeli** (far west).
- **Mani Rimdu** masked dance (Sherpa).

**Institutions**

- **Nepal Academy** (नेपाल प्रज्ञा-प्रतिष्ठान): 2014 BS; language, literature and culture.
- **Nepal Academy of Fine Arts** and **Nepal Academy of Music and Drama:** 2066 BS.

#### 10 · Short-answer question

**Question.** Describe the main styles of traditional architecture in Nepal, with examples.

**Model answer.** Nepal's old buildings follow five main styles. **(1) Pagoda style:** roofs rise one above another, each smaller than the one below, on carved wooden struts. Examples are Pashupatinath, Changu Narayan and the five-roofed Nyatapola of Bhaktapur (1702). The style is Nepal's own gift; Araniko carried it to Tibet and China in the 13th century. **(2) Shikhara style:** a tall tower of stone or brick curves to a point. The stone Krishna Mandir of Patan (1637) and the Mahaboudha are examples. **(3) Stupa or chaitya style:** a solid dome stands on a base and carries a spire; Swayambhunath and Boudhanath are the best known. **(4) Dome style:** rounded roofs, arches and small towers, as in the Janaki Temple of Janakpur. **(5) European style:** the Rana rulers built white palaces with long rows of columns, such as Singha Durbar. **Importance:** these buildings carry the country's history, bring pilgrims and tourists, and keep wood-carvers, metalworkers and masons in work. The earthquake of 2072 showed how easily they can be lost, so rebuilding must use the old materials and skills.

## 6.6 Literature and notable persons (साहित्य र विशिष्ट व्यक्तित्व)

#### 1 · Why it matters

"Who is called the Mahakavi?", "who wrote Muna Madan?", "which book won the Madan Puraskar?" — these are among the most repeated one-mark questions in social and cultural units, and the newest award also counts as current affairs.

#### 2 · Simple explanation

**Literature** (साहित्य) is writing valued as art: poems, plays, stories, novels and essays. Nepali literature in written form grew from religious verse into a modern national literature in about two centuries.

Readers have given **titles of honour** to the great writers, and examiners ask them: Adikavi, Mahakavi, Kavi Shiromani and others. The second thing to learn is the **best-known work** of each writer, and the third is the **awards**.

Nepal also has rich literature in other languages: Maithili, Nepal Bhasa, Limbu and many more.

#### 3 · Key facts

**The great names of Nepali literature**

- **Bhanubhakta Acharya** — **Adikavi** (आदिकवि, the first poet):
  - born on 29 Ashad 1871 BS at Chundi Ramgha, **Tanahun**;
  - put the **Ramayana** into simple Nepali verse; also wrote *Badhu Shiksha* and *Prashnottar*;
  - his birthday is kept as **Bhanu Jayanti** on 29 Ashad.
- **Motiram Bhatta** — **Yuva Kavi** (युवाकवि): found and printed Bhanubhakta's work and wrote his life; brought the ghazal into Nepali.
- **Lekhnath Paudyal** — **Kavi Shiromani** (कविशिरोमणि): born in **Kaski**; *Tarun Tapasi*, *Ritu Bichar*, and the poem *Pinjada ko Suga*.
- **Laxmi Prasad Devkota** — **Mahakavi** (महाकवि):
  - born in Kathmandu on the day of Laxmi Puja, 1966 BS; died in 2016 BS;
  - ***Muna Madan***, in the folk jhyaure metre; the epics ***Shakuntala*** and *Sulochana*; the poem *Pagal*.
- **Balkrishna Sama** — **Natya Samrat** (नाट्यसम्राट, king of drama): *Mukunda Indira*, *Prahlad*; the epic *Chiso Chulho*.
- **Siddhicharan Shrestha** — **Yuga Kavi** (युगकवि): born in **Okhaldhunga**; *Mero Pyaro Okhaldhunga*, *Urvashi*.
- **Madhav Prasad Ghimire** — **Rashtra Kavi** (राष्ट्रकवि): born in **Lamjung**; *Gauri*, *Malati Mangale*.

**Other writers and works**

- **B.P. Koirala:** *Sumnima*, *Tin Ghumti*, *Modiain*.
- **Guru Prasad Mainali:** the story collection *Naso*.
- **Parijat:** the novel ***Shirish ko Phool***; the first woman to win the Madan Puraskar (2022 BS).
- **Bhupi Sherchan:** *Ghumne Mech Mathi Andho Manchhe*.
- **Lil Bahadur Chhetri:** *Basain*. **Diamond Shumsher Rana:** *Seto Bagh*.
- **Jhamak Kumari Ghimire:** *Jiwan Kanda ki Phool* (Madan Puraskar 2067).
- **Krishna Dharabasi:** *Radha* (Madan Puraskar 2062).

**Literature in other languages**

- **Maithili:** the poet **Vidyapati**.
- **Nepal Bhasa:** **Chittadhar Hridaya**, whose epic *Sugat Saurabh* tells the life of the Buddha.
- **Limbu:** the oral scripture **Mundhum**.

**Awards**

- **Madan Puraskar** (मदन पुरस्कार):
  - set up in **2012 BS** by Queen Jagadamba Kumari Devi in memory of her husband, Madan Shumsher;
  - given each year by the **Madan Puraskar Guthi** to the best book in Nepali.
- **Jagadamba Shree** (जगदम्बा-श्री): given by the same Guthi since **2045 BS** for service to Nepali language, literature and culture; the first went to the singer Narayan Gopal.
- 🔄 **For 2081 BS** (announced 6 Bhadra 2082; 22 August 2025): Madan Puraskar to the novel ***Urmal*** by **Chhuden Kabimo**; Jagadamba Shree to Premdev Giri.
- 🔄 **For 2082 BS** (announced 21 Bhadra 2083; 6 September 2026): Madan Puraskar to **Bina Joshi (Rajbhandari)** for her book on the traditional mask dances of the Newar community (*Newar Samudayaka Paramparagat Mukundo Nach*); Jagadamba Shree to the lyricist **Kiran Kharel**.

**Firsts in print and screen**

- First newspaper: **Gorkhapatra**, 1958 BS (Chapter 2).
- First Nepali magazine: *Sudha Sagar*, 1955 BS.
- First film made in Nepal: *Aama*, 2021 BS.

He retold the **Ramayana** in simple, musical Nepali that village people could sing, and wrote *Badhu Shiksha* and *Prashnottar*. His birthday is kept as Bhanu Jayanti, and he is a national hero. **Laxmi Prasad Devkota** (1966–2016 BS, Kathmandu) is the **Mahakavi**, the great poet of modern Nepali. He brought the romantic style, a wide range of subjects and great speed of writing. ***Muna Madan***, in the folk jhyaure metre, tells of a poor man who goes to Lhasa to earn and teaches that a person is great by heart, not by caste. His epics ***Shakuntala*** and *Sulochana*, the poem *Pagal* and his essays set the standard for later writers. **Conclusion:** Bhanubhakta gave Nepali literature its foundation and its unity; Devkota gave it modern form and depth.

## 6.7 World Heritage Sites and places of pilgrimage (विश्व सम्पदा र तीर्थस्थल)

#### 1 · Why it matters

The four World Heritage Sites, the seven monument zones of the Kathmandu Valley and the districts of famous temples are standard questions. The failed bid of Tilaurakot in 2025 and the rebuilding after the earthquake are current affairs.

#### 2 · Simple explanation

**Heritage** (सम्पदा) is what a people has received from the past and wants to keep.

- **Tangible heritage** (मूर्त सम्पदा) can be touched: temples, palaces, stupas, old towns.
- **Intangible heritage** (अमूर्त सम्पदा) lives in people: songs, dances, festivals, skills and foods.

The **World Heritage List** is kept by UNESCO under a convention of 1972. A country first puts a place on its **tentative list**, then sends a full nomination. A committee of states decides each year whether to **inscribe** it, **defer** it or reject it.

A **place of pilgrimage** (तीर्थस्थल) is a holy place that people travel to. In Nepal many are holy to Hindus and Buddhists alike.

#### 3 · Key facts

**The four World Heritage Sites** (Chapter 1)

- **Cultural:** the **Kathmandu Valley** (1979) and **Lumbini**, the birthplace of the Buddha (1997).
- **Natural:** **Sagarmatha National Park** (1979) and **Chitwan National Park** (1984).

**The seven monument zones of the Kathmandu Valley**

- Three palace squares: **Hanuman Dhoka** (Kathmandu), **Patan** and **Bhaktapur** Durbar Squares.
- Two Buddhist stupas: **Swayambhunath** and **Boudhanath**.
- Two Hindu temples: **Pashupatinath** and **Changu Narayan**.
- The Valley was on the List of World Heritage in Danger from 2003 to 2007.

**Lumbini**

- In Rupandehi district. The **Ashoka pillar** of 249 BC marks the birthplace (Chapter 2).
- The Maya Devi Temple and the sacred pond stand beside it.
- The master plan of Lumbini was drawn by the Japanese architect **Kenzo Tange**.

**Waiting for inscription** 🔄

- Nepal's **tentative list has 15 sites**. Among them are Tilaurakot, Ramagrama, Lo Manthang, the Sinja valley, Panauti, the Ram Janaki Temple, Tansen, and the palaces of Gorkha and Nuwakot.
- **Tilaurakot** (Kapilvastu), the old capital of the Shakya where Prince Siddhartha grew up, has been on the tentative list since 1996. Nepal nominated it in 2024.
- At its **47th session in Paris in July 2025**, the World Heritage Committee **deferred** the nomination. Nepal plans to send it again.

**Intangible heritage** 🔄

- Nepal joined UNESCO's convention on intangible cultural heritage in **2010**.
- No report of a Nepali element on UNESCO's Representative List was found when this chapter was checked.
- In July 2025 the Government entered **Yomari**, the Newar rice-flour delicacy, in the national list of intangible heritage.

**Protecting heritage**

- **Ancient Monument Preservation Act, 2013**; the **Department of Archaeology**.
- **Guthi Sansthan** manages the land and rituals of many temples.
- The earthquake of **12 Baisakh 2072 (25 April 2015)** damaged hundreds of monuments, among them Kasthamandap, Dharahara and parts of the three Durbar Squares. Many have since been rebuilt.

**Great places of pilgrimage**

- **Pashupatinath** (Kathmandu): the holiest Shiva temple.
- **Muktinath** (Mustang): holy to Hindus and Buddhists.
- **Janaki Temple** (Janakpurdham, Dhanusha): birthplace of Sita.
- **Barahakshetra** (Sunsari), **Pathibhara** (Taplejung) and **Halesi Mahadev** (Khotang).
- **Manakamana** (Gorkha), **Gosainkunda** (Rasuwa) and **Devghat** (at the meeting of the Kali Gandaki and Trishuli).
- **Swargadwari** (Pyuthan) and **Ridi** (Gulmi–Palpa).
- **Khaptad** and **Badimalika** (Sudurpashchim); **Chandannath** (Jumla).
- **Four dhams of Nepal:** Pashupat Kshetra, Mukti Kshetra, Ruru Kshetra (Ridi) and Baraha Kshetra.

#### 10 · Short-answer question

**Question.** Name the World Heritage Sites of Nepal and explain why their conservation matters.

**Model answer.** Nepal has **four World Heritage Sites**. Two are cultural: the **Kathmandu Valley** (1979), with seven monument zones — Hanuman Dhoka, Patan and Bhaktapur Durbar Squares, Swayambhunath, Boudhanath, Pashupatinath and Changu Narayan — and **Lumbini** (1997), the birthplace of the Buddha. Two are natural: **Sagarmatha National Park** (1979) and **Chitwan National Park** (1984). Fifteen more places, including Tilaurakot, wait on the tentative list; Tilaurakot's nomination was deferred in 2025. **Why conservation matters:** (1) the sites are proof of Nepal's history, art and faith; (2) they give the country a name in the world; (3) they bring tourists and pilgrims and so give work; (4) the parks protect rare animals, forests and the sources of rivers; (5) festivals and daily worship at the monuments keep communities together. **Threats:** earthquakes, as in 2072; crowded building around the monuments; pollution; theft of images; and climate change in the mountains. **Measures:** follow the Ancient Monument Preservation Act and UNESCO's rules; rebuild with traditional materials and skills; give local communities and guthi a share in management; control building in the buffer areas; and record both tangible and intangible heritage.

## 6.8 National symbols, heroes, honours and days (राष्ट्रिय प्रतीक, विभूति, विभूषण र दिवस)

#### 1 · Why it matters

The national flower, bird and anthem, the number of national heroes and the dates of national days are among the first facts any General Knowledge paper asks. Bagmati's syllabus names "national days" in so many words. Two changes of 2082–2083 — a second weekly holiday and a new martyrs' day — are current affairs.

#### 2 · Simple explanation

**National symbols** (राष्ट्रिय प्रतीक) are signs by which a country is known: its flag, anthem, coat of arms, flower, colour, animal and bird. In Nepal they are fixed by the **Constitution** itself (Articles 8 and 9; Schedules 1 to 3), so they can be changed only by amending it (Chapter 3).

A **national hero** (राष्ट्रिय विभूति) is a person of the past whom the State honours for giving the nation its pride. The title is given only after death.

**Honours** (विभूषण) are titles, orders and medals given by the State to living persons for their service. A **national day** (राष्ट्रिय दिवस) recalls an event or a cause every year; some national days are public holidays.

#### 3 · Key facts

**Symbols in the Constitution**

- **Flag** (Article 8; Schedule 1): two triangles; crimson with a deep-blue border; a white moon above and a white sun with twelve rays below (Chapter 3). It is the only national flag in the world that is not a rectangle.
- **Anthem** (Article 9 (1); Schedule 2): **"Sayaun Thunga Phulka"** (सयौँ थुँगा फूलका).
  - words by **Byakul Maila**; music by **Amber Gurung**;
  - adopted on **18 Shrawan 2064 (3 August 2007)**; chosen from 1,292 entries;
  - the earlier anthem was "Shriman Gambhir".
- **Coat of arms** (Article 9 (2); Schedule 3): it shows the flag, Sagarmatha, hills, the Terai, a man's and a woman's hands joined, a garland of rhododendron and the map of Nepal. The second amendment changed the map in it (Chapter 3).
- **Article 9 (3):**
  - national flower — **rhododendron** (लालीगुराँस);
  - national colour — **crimson** (सिम्रिक);
  - national animal — **cow**;
  - national bird — **danphe** (डाँफे, the lophophorus).
- **Not in the Constitution:** the national sport is **volleyball**, declared by the Government on 9 Jestha 2074 (23 May 2017).

**National heroes** 🔄

- Nepal has **18** national heroes.
- The first **13** were named in the time of King Mahendra on the advice of a committee led by Balkrishna Sama. Books give different years for this.
- Five were added later: **Shankhadhar Sakhwa** (2056 BS), **Pasang Lhamu Sherpa** (2059 BS — the first woman among them), **Mahaguru Phalgunanda** (2066 BS), **Bhakti Thapa** (2078 BS) and **Jay Prithvi Bahadur Singh** (decision of **6 Ashad 2079; 20 June 2022**).
- The Council of Ministers decides who is to be named.

**Honours**

- **Article 277:** the **President** confers titles, honours and decorations on behalf of the State. No citizen may accept an honour from a foreign government without the approval of the Government of Nepal.
- The highest honour is the **Nepal Ratna** (नेपालरत्न).
- The yearly list is usually announced on **Constitution Day**.

**National days**

- **11 Baisakh** — Loktantra Day (the people's movement of 2062/63).
- **15 Jestha** — Republic Day (गणतन्त्र दिवस).
- **3 Ashoj** — **Constitution Day**, kept as the National Day.
- **27 Poush** — Prithvi Jayanti and National Unity Day.
- **16 Magh** — Martyrs' Day (सहिद दिवस).
- **7 Falgun** — Democracy Day (प्रजातन्त्र दिवस).
- Others: Law Day (26 Baisakh), day against caste discrimination (21 Jestha), Paddy Day (15 Ashad), Bhanu Jayanti (29 Ashad), Civil Service Day (22 Bhadra), Children's Day (29 Bhadra), Tax Day (1 Mangsir), Earthquake Safety Day (2 Magh), Cooperative Day (20 Chaitra).
- 🔄 **23 Bhadra — Gen Z Martyrs' Day** (जेन-जी सहिद दिवस), first placed in the holiday list for 2083. It recalls the movement that began on 23 Bhadra 2082 (8 September 2025).

**Public holidays** 🔄

- The list of holidays for 2083 was approved by the Council of Ministers in Falgun 2082 (February 2026). It has **37 public holidays** besides the weekly holiday.
- **Two weekly holidays:** on 22 Chaitra 2082 (5 April 2026) the Council of Ministers decided that government offices and educational institutions would close on **Saturday and Sunday**. Until then only Saturday was the weekly holiday.
- On 4 Shrawan 2083 (20 July 2026) a task force was formed to review the other public holidays, because offices were now closed on more days. Its result had not been seen when this chapter was checked.

## 6.9 Social issues and inclusion (सामाजिक समस्या र समावेशीकरण)

#### 1 · Why it matters

Untouchability, chhaupadi, child marriage and the rights of women, children, the elderly and persons with disabilities are named in "social condition" units and are a rich source of matching questions on Acts and Articles. They are also essay topics in the later papers.

#### 2 · Simple explanation

A **social problem** (सामाजिक समस्या) is a custom or condition that harms many people and that society has agreed to change. Some come from old beliefs — untouchability, chhaupadi, accusations of witchcraft. Some come from poverty and inequality — child labour, trafficking, child marriage.

The law answers in two ways:

- it **forbids and punishes** the harmful act;
- it gives the weaker group **rights and a share** in schools, jobs and public bodies. This second way is **inclusion** (समावेशीकरण).

**Social security** (सामाजिक सुरक्षा) is the State's help to people who cannot fully support themselves: the old, single women, persons with disabilities, children of the poorest groups and endangered communities.

#### 3 · Key facts

**Old reforms** (Chapter 2)

- Sati was abolished in 1977 BS and slavery in 1981 BS.
- The Muluki Ain of 2020 BS ended caste discrimination in law.

**Untouchability and caste discrimination** (छुवाछुत)

- **Article 24:** no untouchability or discrimination in any private or public place.
- **21 Jestha 2063 (4 June 2006):** Nepal was declared a nation free of caste-based untouchability. The day is kept each year as the national day against caste discrimination.
- **Caste-based Discrimination and Untouchability (Offence and Punishment) Act, 2068.**
- **Article 40:** rights of Dalit; **National Dalit Commission** (Article 255).

**Harmful practices**

- **Chhaupadi** (छाउपडी) — keeping a woman in a shed during menstruation, mostly in Karnali and Sudurpashchim. The Criminal Code of 2074, in force from 1 Bhadra 2075 (17 August 2018), makes it an offence: up to three months in prison, a fine of up to Rs 3,000, or both.
- **Child marriage:** the legal age of marriage is **20 years** for both men and women 🔄. In the census the average age of women at first marriage is 18.
- **Dowry** (दाइजो): asking for it is an offence.
- **Witchcraft accusation:** Act of **2072**.
- **Domestic violence:** Act of **2066**.
- **Human trafficking:** Act of **2064**; Article 29 forbids trafficking, slavery and forced work.
- **Bonded labour:** kamaiya (2057), haliya (2065) and kamlari were freed (Chapter 5); the harawa–charawa of the Terai were declared free in 2079 BS.

**Groups and their rights**

- **Women:** Article 38 — equal right to family property, safe motherhood, protection from violence, and a share in all State bodies. At least one third of the members of Parliament must be women. **National Women Commission** (Article 252).
- **Children:** Article 39. The **Act Relating to Children, 2075** defines a child as a person **below 18 years**.
- **Senior citizens:** Article 41. The **Senior Citizens Act, 2063** counts a person of **60 years or more** as a senior citizen.
- **Persons with disabilities:** Act of **2074**. It names ten kinds of disability and four degrees, shown by the colour of the identity card: **red** (profound), **blue** (severe), **yellow** (moderate) and **white** (mild). The census found a disability in 2.2 percent of the people.
- **Social justice:** **Article 42** gives disadvantaged groups the right to take part in State bodies on the principle of inclusion. **Article 43** gives the right to social security.
- **State policy:** Article 51 (j) lists the policies on social justice and inclusion.

**Tools of inclusion**

- Seven constitutional commissions: Women, Dalit, Inclusion, Indigenous Nationalities, Madhesi, Tharu and Muslim (Articles 252–264).
- Proportional seats in elections for women, Dalit, indigenous nationalities, Khas Arya, Madhesi, Tharu, Muslims and backward regions (Article 84).
- 45 percent of civil service posts filled by open competition are reserved (Chapter 4).
- 🔄 Social security allowances reach about 3.64 million people (Chapter 5).

#### 10 · Short-answer question

**Question.** What is caste-based untouchability? State the legal provisions against it and suggest how it can be ended in practice.

**Model answer.** **Untouchability** (छुवाछुत) is the practice of treating persons of certain castes as impure — refusing to share water, food, a house, a temple or marriage with them. It is a crime against human dignity and has kept Dalit communities poor and excluded. **Legal provisions:** (1) the Muluki Ain of 2020 BS removed caste discrimination from the law; (2) on 21 Jestha 2063 Nepal was declared a nation free of untouchability; (3) **Article 24** of the Constitution forbids untouchability and discrimination in any private or public place and makes it punishable, and **Article 40** gives Dalit special rights in education, health, employment and land; (4) the **Act of 2068** punishes the offence with imprisonment and fine and gives compensation to the victim; (5) the **National Dalit Commission** watches over these rights. **Why it continues:** old beliefs, poverty and landlessness of Dalit families, weak complaints and slow prosecution, and social pressure to "settle" cases. **How to end it:** quick registration and prosecution of every case; legal aid and protection for victims; education and scholarships; land, skills and jobs for Dalit households; inter-caste social events and support for inter-caste marriage; and action by ward offices, schools and religious leaders. **Conclusion:** the law is clear; practice changes only when the State enforces it and neighbours stop accepting it.

## 6.10 Education (शिक्षा)

#### 1 · Why it matters

Bagmati has a unit called "Education and health" with four questions. In the other provinces the literacy rate, the first school and the first university appear in the social unit, and education laws appear in current affairs.

#### 2 · Simple explanation

**Literacy** (साक्षरता) means that a person can read and write with understanding. The census measures it for everyone aged five and above.

School education has two levels:

- **basic education** (आधारभूत शिक्षा) — one year of early childhood education and **Grades 1 to 8**;
- **secondary education** (माध्यमिक शिक्षा) — **Grades 9 to 12**.

Above the school come universities and technical institutes. Under the Constitution, basic and secondary education are a power of the **local level** (Schedule 8), while universities and standards are federal and provincial matters.

#### 3 · Key facts

**Right and law**

- **Article 31:** every citizen has the right of access to basic education; basic education is **compulsory and free**, and education up to the **secondary level is free**.
- **Act Relating to Compulsory and Free Education, 2075.**
- **Education Act, 2028** — still the main school law. Its eighth amendment (2073) made the two levels: basic (1–8) and secondary (9–12).
- 🔄 **School Education Bill:** a Bill to replace the Act of 2028 was registered in Parliament in Bhadra 2080 but did not become law. In Bhadra 2083 the Education Minister, Sasmit Pokharel, called a new school education Act the Government's first priority. No new Act had been passed when this chapter was checked.
- Policy papers: National Education Policy, 2076; School Education Sector Plan, 2022–2032.

**Literacy in the Census of 2078**

- **76.2 percent** of people aged five and above are literate (65.9 percent in 2068).
- **Men 83.6 percent; women 69.4 percent** — a gap of 14.2 points.
- Municipalities 78.5 percent; rural municipalities 71.9 percent.
- **Highest province:** Bagmati, 82.1 percent. **Lowest:** Madhesh, 63.5 percent.
- **Highest district:** **Kathmandu**, 89.23 percent. **Lowest:** **Rautahat**, 57.75 percent.
- 🔄 **"Literate" districts:** a district is declared literate when 95 percent of its people aged 15 to 60 can read and write. By Bhadra 2082, **67 districts** had been declared literate. The ten still waiting were eight districts of Madhesh, with Kalikot and Dolpa.

**Examinations and bodies**

- **Grade 10:** Secondary Education Examination (SEE), which replaced the School Leaving Certificate.
- **Grade 12:** examination of the **National Examinations Board** (राष्ट्रिय परीक्षा बोर्ड).
- Results are given in letter grades.
- **Curriculum Development Centre:** school curriculum and textbooks.
- **University Grants Commission** (2050 BS): funds and oversees universities.
- **Council for Technical Education and Vocational Training** (2045 BS): technical schools.
- **Medical Education Commission** (2075 BS): medical education.

**History**

- **Durbar High School**, 1910 BS: the first school, at first only for the ruling family.
- **Tri-Chandra College**, 1975 BS: the first college.
- **School Leaving Certificate Board**, 1990 BS.
- **Tribhuvan University**, **2016 BS**: the first university.
- **National Education System Plan**, 2028 BS.
- **Nepal Sanskrit University**, 2043 BS; **Kathmandu University**, 2048 BS.

**Higher education today** 🔄

- By Falgun 2082 Nepal had **21 universities** — **15 federal and 6 provincial** — and **8 health-science academies** (Economic Survey 2082/83).
- Tribhuvan University remains by far the largest.

**School calendar** 🔄

- From the school year 2083, schools close on **Saturday and Sunday**, and the long vacation was cut from 45 to 30 days.
- About 11 percent of the federal budget goes to education, the Minister said in 2083.

#### 10 · Short-answer question

**Question.** Describe the structure of school education in Nepal and its main problems.

**Model answer.** **Structure.** School education has two levels: **basic** (one year of early childhood education and Grades 1–8) and **secondary** (Grades 9–12). Article 31 makes basic education compulsory and free and secondary education free; the Act of 2075 carries this out. Under Schedule 8 the local level runs basic and secondary schools. Pupils sit a basic-level examination at Grade 8, the Secondary Education Examination at Grade 10 and the National Examinations Board's examination at Grade 12. The main school law is still the Education Act of 2028. **Achievements.** Literacy rose from 65.9 percent in 2068 to 76.2 percent in 2078, and 67 districts have been declared literate. **Problems:** (1) quality is weak — many pupils cannot read or count at grade level; (2) gaps are wide: women 69.4 percent against men 83.6 percent, Madhesh 63.5 percent against Bagmati 82.1 percent; (3) many children leave before Grade 10; (4) community schools lack teachers of science, mathematics and English; (5) the roles of the three levels of government are unclear because the new school Act is delayed; (6) education receives only about a tenth of the budget; (7) schooling is weakly linked with skills and jobs. **Way forward:** pass the school education Act, train and manage teachers better, support girls and poor children to stay in school, and add technical education.

### D. Five short-answer questions

**1. What is the demographic dividend? Is Nepal in a position to gain from it?**
*Points for the answer:* a period when the working-age share of the population is large and the share of dependants small · Census of 2078: 61.96 percent aged 15–59; children 27.83 percent and falling; seniors 10.21 percent and rising · growth only 0.92 percent, so the window will close as the population ages · at present much of the young workforce is abroad (about 2.19 million absent) and unemployment at home is high · to gain: skills and technical education, jobs in farming, industry, tourism and information technology, health, and productive use of remittance · otherwise the country grows old before it grows rich

**2. Describe the main festivals of Nepal and their social importance.**
*Points for the answer:* national festivals — Dashain (15 days, Ashoj), Tihar (5 days, Kartik) · regional and community festivals — Chhath, Maghi, Lhosar (Tamu, Sonam, Gyalpo), Ubhauli and Udhauli, Teej, Gaura, Jitiya, Buddha Jayanti, Eid, Christmas · jatras of the Kathmandu Valley — Indra Jatra, Gai Jatra, Rato Machhindranath, Bisket · importance: family reunion; rest after farm work; respect for elders, sisters and nature; markets for goods and crafts; tourism; shared holidays that bind communities · concerns: costly display, alcohol and gambling, pollution, loss of old forms

**3. What steps has Nepal taken for social inclusion?**
*Points for the answer:* Preamble and Article 4 — an inclusive State · Article 42 right to social justice; Articles 38–41 for women, children, Dalit and senior citizens; Article 43 social security · proportional representation and reserved clusters in elections (Article 84); one third women in Parliament · 45 percent reservation in the civil service · seven constitutional commissions (Articles 252–264) · laws against untouchability, domestic violence, trafficking and witchcraft accusations · provincial official languages; mother-tongue education · social security allowances · gaps: benefits taken by the better-off within each group; weak data; slow enforcement

**4. What did the earthquake of 2072 teach about protecting cultural heritage?**
*Points for the answer:* 12 Baisakh 2072 (25 April 2015) · hundreds of monuments damaged, including Kasthamandap, Dharahara and parts of the three Durbar Squares · rebuilding with traditional wood, brick and craftsmen; the rebuilt Dharahara opened on 3 Ashoj 2081 · lessons: keep drawings and records of every monument; strengthen old buildings before a disaster; train craftsmen and keep the guthi system alive; involve local communities; control modern building around monuments; follow the Ancient Monument Preservation Act and UNESCO's guidance · heritage is also intangible — festivals and skills must continue

**5. Why is the literacy of women lower than that of men in Nepal? Suggest measures.**
*Points for the answer:* Census of 2078: men 83.6 percent, women 69.4 percent; gap 14.2 points; women's literacy is lowest in Madhesh (54.7 percent) · causes: preference for sons; early marriage (average age of women at first marriage 18); household and farm work; distance and safety; lack of toilets and women teachers; poverty; older women never went to school · measures: scholarships and midday meals; enforcing the marriage age of 20; girls' toilets and women teachers; adult literacy classes linked with income; campaigns in the ten districts not yet declared literate; local governments held to account under Article 31

### E. Common mistakes

1. Reading a **sex ratio of 95.59** as "more men". It means 95.59 men for every 100 women.
2. Quoting the **preliminary** population, 29,192,480. The final figure is 29,164,578.
3. Mixing **142** (castes and ethnic groups), **124** (mother tongues) and **59** (listed indigenous nationalities).
4. Calling the **Brahman** the largest group. The Chhetri are.
5. Saying that **Article 6** makes Nepali the only language of the nation.
6. Swapping **Tamu** (Gurung), **Sonam** (Tamang) and **Gyalpo** (Sherpa) Lhosar.
7. Counting the **Kirat** religion inside Hindu or Buddhist.
8. Calling the **Krishna Mandir** of Patan a pagoda. It is shikhara style.
9. Swapping **Adikavi** (Bhanubhakta) and **Mahakavi** (Devkota).
10. Saying that the **Nepal Academy** gives the Madan Puraskar. The Madan Puraskar Guthi does.
11. Counting the **Kathmandu Valley** as seven World Heritage Sites. It is one site with seven zones.
12. Writing that **Tilaurakot** is on the World Heritage List. It was deferred in 2025.
13. Placing **volleyball** in Article 9.
14. Writing **16 or 17** national heroes. There are 18.
15. Giving **2068** as the year Nepal was declared free of untouchability. The declaration was on 21 Jestha 2063; the Act came in 2068.
16. Writing that **basic education** means Grades 1–5. It is Grades 1–8.
